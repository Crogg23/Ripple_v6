Ripple · a look at the public record

The government wrote it all down.
Then nothing happened.

Every number below comes from the government's own files — inspections it ran, tests it ordered, bans it issued. We didn't leak anything. We just put two public records side by side and checked whether they agreed.

Five times we checked. Five times they didn't.

1Your tap water

31.2 million people are drinking from water systems that tested over the lead limit — with no violation on record.

31.2M
people served by those systems

There are 3,898 public water systems in this country where a lead test came back above the federal safety level, and the enforcement file next to it is empty. Not "under review." Empty.

That's more people than live in Texas.

The honest catchGoing over the lead limit doesn't automatically make a water system a lawbreaker — it's supposed to trigger a required fix. The point isn't that 3,898 crimes happened. It's that the test result and the paper trail don't match, at a scale of 31 million people.
What we got wrong firstWe caught our own mistake here first: our original number was 35 million, until we noticed some readings were recorded in the wrong unit — off by a factor of a thousand. Ohio's numbers were almost entirely that error. We threw those out and re-ran it. 31.2 million is what's left after the correction.
2Where we put our parents

About 1 in 4 five-star nursing homes is carrying a stack of fire-code violations.

728
five-star homes with 16+ fire-safety citations

The government gives every nursing home a rating out of five stars. Families use it exactly how you'd think — five stars means "this is the good one."

Of the 3,039 homes holding a perfect five-star rating, 728 of them have 16 or more fire-safety citations on file. Same count as some of the worst-rated homes in the country, sitting underneath a perfect score.

The honest catchBefore you panic: 96% of those citations are the lowest severity band — blocked-door, bad-paperwork stuff, not a building about to go up. Read this as "the star rating isn't telling you everything," not "728 death traps."
What we got wrong firstWe checked whether the government already flags this somewhere else, which would make it old news. It doesn't — 95% of those same homes also score 4 or 5 stars on the inspection-specific score. Nothing the public sees catches it.
3The factory down the road

15,323 facilities broke environmental rules every single quarter for three years straight. Most of them were never actually punished.

82%
of repeat violators got no formal penalty

The EPA tracks compliance quarter by quarter. So we asked a simple question: who failed every quarter, for three years, without exception?

15,323 facilities. And across the wider group of repeat offenders — 54,315 of them — 44,319 never received a formal enforcement action. They got letters. Letters are free.

The honest catchThe obvious objection: maybe these are trivial paperwork slips, and a warning letter is the right response. We checked the actual violation status on all 44,319. 86% are logged as real violations or significant violations — not blank rows, not filing errors.
4Where your donation goes

19,043 organizations lost their tax-exempt status and are still sitting on the IRS's own "safe to donate" list.

19,043
revoked, never reinstated, still listed as safe

The IRS publishes a list of charities you can safely give to and take the deduction. It separately publishes a list of organizations whose status it revoked.

Those two lists overlap on 125,692 organizations. Most of that is fine — 106,649 were revoked and later restored, exactly how it should work. The problem is the other 19,043: revoked, never restored, still on the list telling you they're good.

What we got wrong firstThe innocent explanation is that the two files are just out of sync and it'll catch up. We tested that directly: if it were lag, these would cluster in the last year or two. They don't. There's a wave in 2010 and a steady stream every year since. A charity revoked fifteen years ago is still listed as safe to donate to today.
5The doctor's office

439 doctors banned from Medicare for fraud kept receiving payments from drug and device companies afterward.

439
banned providers still receiving industry money

The federal government maintains a list of doctors barred from billing Medicare or Medicaid — usually for fraud. Being on it is about as serious as medical discipline gets.

439 of them show up receiving drug-company and device-company money in the years after their ban. Not a fortune each — roughly $232,000 total across all of them, made mostly of industry meals, consulting fees, and speaking payments.

The honest catchTaking industry money while banned isn't illegal on its own — the ban stops you from billing federal programs, not from having a relationship with a manufacturer. This is a smell, not a crime.
What we got wrong firstOur first version of this number was $1 million, and the headline was one doctor pulling in $620,000 after his ban. We looked at what those payments actually were: patent royalties. He'd invented something, and the license payments kept running on autopilot. That's a completely different story than a banned doctor on the take — so we pulled it out of the headline and the number dropped to $232,000. The smaller number is the true one.

How to read this without getting fooled — including by us

Big numbers are easy to produce and easy to get wrong. So before publishing any of this, we went back through every finding and actively tried to break it — looking for the boring explanation that would make it go away.

Twice, the boring explanation won. Our lead-in-water number was too high because some lab results were recorded in the wrong unit. Our banned-doctor headline was built on one man's patent royalties, which isn't the scandal it looked like. Both are corrected above, in plain sight, with the original numbers left in so you can see what moved.

Three findings survived everything we threw at them. Those are the ones worth being angry about.
