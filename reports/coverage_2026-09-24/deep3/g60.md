## 1. ENERGY__INTL_EMBER_ELEC: **live**

369,264 rows. One row = one measure (like coal TWh) for one country or region in one year, 2000-2025.
One load run. No duplicate keys (country + year + measure). 66 measures.

**The lead: in 2025 the US was the world's coal growth market**

```
                           2024 TWh   2025 TWh   change
US coal                      652.2      737.2    +85.0  (+13.0%)
US gas                     1,869.9    1,807.3    -62.6
US wind + solar              755.7      853.2    +97.6
US demand                  4,404.8    4,535.8   +131.0
World coal                10,539.8   10,490.5    -49.3
World minus US coal        9,887.6    9,753.3   -134.3
```

- **Peer check.** 89 countries have coal numbers for both 2024 and 2025.
  - 23 others had a coal rise. Together those rises add up to 36.3 TWh. **The US alone added 2.3 times that.**
  - The 28 countries that made at least 10 TWh of coal power in 2024: median change **+0.6%**.
  - In percent, the US ranks 3rd, behind Pakistan (+18.7%, from 24.3 TWh) and Chile (+15.9%, from 13.6 TWh). In TWh it's first. Pakistan is second at +4.6.
  - China -55.3 TWh, India -43.8.
- **The US rise cancels 63% of the coal cut everywhere else** (+85.0 against -134.3).
- **Power CO2:** US **+52.1 Mt** (+3.1%). The other 90 reporting countries: -103.5 Mt. World: -21.8 Mt.
- **Time check.** US coal's biggest one-year rises since 2000:
  - 2021: +124.6. A rebound after 2020's -191.6.
  - 2010: +91.4. A rebound after 2009's -229.9.
  - **2025: +85.0. It follows a year that was already down (-23.0), so it's not a rebound.**
- Straight from the raw rows (S15), not only the grid: the VALUE and YOY columns agree.

**Hit means:** in Ember's numbers, the US burned more coal in 2025 while the world burned less. That's the biggest non-rebound jump in 25 years.
**Miss would have meant:** US coal flat or down like the median coal country (+0.6%). That's not what the data shows.

**Dull explanation: gas prices.** Plants burn coal instead of gas when gas gets pricey.
- **Tested, and it fits.** US gas power fell 62.6 TWh in the same year coal rose 85.0.
- Demand grew 131.0 TWh. Wind and solar covered 97.6 of it.
- So this reads as coal replacing gas, plus a slice of new demand. It's not a coal revival built on new plants.
- **Not tested:** federal emergency orders that kept retiring coal plants running in 2025. The table can't split that out.

**Limits:**
- ⚠ **2025 is partial.** Only 91 countries have 2025 rows. They made 93.5% of 2024's power.
- 2025 has 51 of the 66 measures (no capacity). Ember estimates the World row for 2025.
- 2025 figures are early estimates built from monthly data. Final annual numbers may move them. **Not checked against EIA.**
- The US 2025 coal rise has been reported before (EIA, Ember). What's new here is the scale: 2.3x every other rise combined, and 63% of the world's cut cancelled.

**Checked and parked: "who got dirtier, 2015 to 2024"**
- 110 countries with at least 10 TWh of demand. Fossil share fell by a median 5.4 points.
- It rose in 13. **In 11 of the 13, hydro's share fell at least 2 points.** That's drought or dams, not a policy shift.
  - Ghana -14.3 hydro points, Cambodia -19.9, Myanmar -12.0, Zambia -10.3, Laos -9.5.
- Of the other two, Taiwan's nuclear phase-out explains it (-9.9 nuclear points). Cuba is still at 96% fossil.
- **The coal-builders in Asia** (coal-share change 2015→2024, Asia median 0.0 across 29 countries):
  - Philippines +19.4 points (44.5% → 63.9%). Bangladesh +18.2. Vietnam +15.5. Pakistan +15.3.
  - These are known stories. The Philippines stands out: its CO2 per kWh went 546 → 630 g while Asia's median fell 29.5 g.

## 2. ENERGY__FED_EIA860_3_4_ENERGY_STORAGE: probed

786 rows: 777 batteries, 5 flywheels, 3 solar-thermal storage units, 1 compressed-air unit.
Batteries: 27,258.5 MW. **All 786 land in the master generator table (3_1)** on plant + generator.

**Lead: building-housed batteries are rare, fading, and two-thirds sit at two sites**
- The enclosure codes are CS 679, CT 59, BL 36, OT 9, blank 3.
  - I read BL = building and CS = container from EIA's form. **There's no code lookup in the warehouse. Not verified.**
  - One check fits: PG&E's Elkhorn battery (Tesla cabinets at the same Moss Landing site) is coded CS.
- 35 batteries in buildings, **1,482.4 MW = 5.4%** of battery MW.
- **Moss Landing (plant 260, 3 units, 750 MW) + Gateway (plant 63834, 250 MW) = 1,000 MW = 67.5%** of building MW. Also 3,700 of 4,651 MWh.
- There are 112 battery units of 100 MW or more. **5 are in buildings, at 3 sites:** Moss Landing ×3, Gateway, AES Alamitos (100 MW).
- **Time check:**
  - Buildings held 21.5% of battery MW for units started 2021 or earlier. For 2022-2024 starts it's 1.9%, and 0.3% without Moss Landing's 2023 unit.
  - 2024: one building unit, 1.6 MW, out of 11,157 MW added that year.
- **Peer check:** California 10.5% in buildings (1,233.6 of 11,768.6 MW). Every other state together: 1.6%.
- **Why it could be a story:** public reports say Gateway burned in May 2024 and Moss Landing's 300 MW unit burned in January 2025.
  - ⚠ **Those fires are not in the warehouse.** I did not check them here.
  - If they hold up: 2 of the 3 sites with 100 MW+ battery buildings had major fires within 8 months.
- **Hit means:** the housing type at both reported fire sites was already rare (5.4% of battery MW), and builders had almost stopped using it (1.9% since 2022).
- **What it can't say:** whether buildings are riskier. Two fires don't make a rate. Container sites burn too; Elkhorn had a fire in 2022 (public reporting, not checked).
- **Dull explanation:** early big projects reused old power-plant buildings (Moss Landing is a gas-plant site). Newer ones are almost all containers (98% of MW since 2022). **The reuse part is not tested.**

**Also checked (known trends, not leads):**
- Storage hours (MWh per MW): **California 3.42, Texas 1.43.** Texas units are short-duration.
- Main use (every battery but one lists exactly one "PR" use):
  - Arbitrage (charge cheap, sell dear) went from 6% of MW (units started 2016 or earlier) to 47% (2024).
  - Frequency regulation fell from 44% to 17%.
- 306 batteries say they directly back up another unit. 275 of them back solar. Only one backs a gas plant: Fresno Cogen, 16 MW, backing an 18.6 MW turbine.

## 3. ENERGY__FED_EIA860_3_2_WIND: probed

1,563 wind generators, 153,489 MW. Statuses: OP 1,541, OS 18, OA 4.
**All 1,563 land in the master generator table**, and MW agree on every row.

**Checked: the oldest turbines and who plans to replace them**
- "Legacy" here means the generator started in 2004 or earlier and its turbines average under 1 MW each.
  - 91 generators, **4,820 turbines, 2,240.4 MW (1.46% of US wind MW)**.
  - California has 706.8 MW of them, 10.9% of its wind. Iowa 419.1, Minnesota 301.2, Texas 276.8 (0.7% of TX).
  - Biggest: FPL Energy Vansycle, WA + OR, 454 Vestas V47s. Storm Lake 1, IA, 138 Zond Z-50s. Mojave 3/4/5, CA, 300 Mitsubishi MWT-250s.
- **Only 1 of the 91 has a planned repower year. 5 have a planned retirement year.**
- Meanwhile, 40 generators (3,999 MW) have repowers planned. **Their median start year is 2009.**
- So the replacement money is going to 15-year-old farms, not the oldest.
- **Dull explanation:** tax credits reward partly repowering a 10-year-old farm more than tearing down tiny 1990s machines. **Not tested.** It's how the credit works, not something in the table.

**Checked: out-of-service turbines by maker size**
- 22 generators aren't running (OS/OA).
- "Big maker" = GE, Vestas, Siemens, Siemens Gamesa, Gamesa, Nordex.
- Started 2010 or earlier: small makers 8 of 200 down (4.0%), big makers 5 of 390 (1.3%).
- Started after 2010: small makers 6 of 150 (4.0%), big makers 3 of 823 (0.4%).
- The down list is full of makers that left the market: DeWind, Clipper, NEG-Micon, Fuhrlander, Leitwind, Unison.
- **Only 22 units. It's a hint, not a finding.**

**Time:** 2024 added 5,156 MW (31 generators). The lowest year since 2014 (4,955); 2020 added 14,878.
Older years undercount: retired units are gone, and repowered units keep their old start year (see traps).

## ⚠ Traps for the ledger

1. **EIA-860 tables are the "Operable" sheet only.** Wind, storage and the master generator table (3_1) hold only OP/SB/OS/OA.
   - No proposed units, no retired ones: 3_1 has 26,855 rows, all one of those four codes.
   - "Every wind generator in 2024" means every one still on the operable list, including 22 that are out of service.
   - 11 batteries (824.2 MW) say they back a generator that isn't in the operable list. It's probably a solar farm not yet built, but the missing proposed sheet means **not tested.**
2. **Three big batteries report one container's specs:** Daggett 2 (131 MW), Daggett 3 (149 MW) and Waiawa Solar (36 MW).
   - Each shows **4.6 MWh and a 2.3 MW charge rate** on 316 MW of batteries. The real MWh isn't in the warehouse.
   - Sky Ranch Solar: 50 MW nameplate, but 200 MW max charge and discharge.
3. **Pumped hydro isn't in the storage table.** 144 operating units (20,987 MW) live only in 3_1 as prime mover PS. That's EIA's design. But "US storage" built from this table misses 43% of storage MW: 20,987 pumped-hydro MW out of 48,808 combined.
4. **Wind OPERATING_YEAR is the original start, not the turbines' age.**
   - 55 generators that started in 2004 or earlier have turbines averaging 1.5 MW or more (2,434 MW).
   - Some rows mix old and new fields:
     - Mesa Wind, 1984: model "V15", hub 80 ft, but 8 turbines at 4.2 MW each.
     - Klondike, 2002: model "V150-4.2", hub 344.5 ft, but 16 turbines at 1.53 MW.
   - Maker names need cleaning: "VESTAS" ×2 vs "Vestas" ×360. Siemens, Siemens Gamesa and Gamesa are separate labels.
5. **Ember VALUE mixes 7 units:** TWh, %, GW, mtCO2, gCO2/kWh, MWh.
   - The glance's "sum 24.8M, top 1% hold 61%" adds percentages to terawatt-hours. Meaningless.
   - 13 region rows (World, EU, G20, OECD, Asia…) sit next to the countries. Countries add up to World within 0.4% through 2024. Summing everything double-counts.
6. **Ember IS_EU, IS_OECD, IS_G20 are empty on every row.** S03 counted 0 filled. The catalog says IS_G7 and IS_ASEAN are empty too; I didn't recount those.
7. **Ember 2025 is partial** (see section 1): 91 countries, 51 measures, no capacity. 2024 also drops 16 small territories that 2023 had (9.2 TWh).
   - Also seen, **not checked:** coal capacity factors come out at 97% for South Korea in 2015 and 88% for Japan. The GW and TWh series may not line up. Check before dividing one by the other.

Side note on a table outside this group: in `ECONOMICS__FED_US_SEC_EDGAR`, **JPMorgan is 25,351 of 48,984 rows (52%)**. Count per company before ranking anything there.

parked: Applied Materials shows STATEOFINCORPORATION = DC in both SEC tables, though its EDGAR name ends "/DE". I didn't check EDGAR itself.
