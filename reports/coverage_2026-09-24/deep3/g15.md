## 1. JUDGE_IDEOLOGY_COA: probed (the spin-off lead is live-grade)

It has 703 appeals judges. Each row is a name, a circuit (1-11, 12 = DC, 13 = Federal) and a JCS score.

**Checked: what the score really is**
- 703 rows and 287 distinct scores.
  - 174 judges have a score of their own.
  - 314 judges share a score with at least 4 others.
  - The top values: 0.693 ×28, -0.358 ×28, 0.692 ×26, 0.403 ×22.
- That fits how JCS is built. The judge gets the appointing president's score, or the home-state senators' score when senatorial courtesy applied. **It measures who appointed the judge, not how the judge rules.**
- The 12 repeated names are judges moved from the 5th Circuit to the new 11th in 1981 (Tjoflat, Fay, Hill, Kravitch and others). Each one has two rows with the same score.

**Checked: the join to FJC**
- Joined on surname + circuit to `FED_FJC_SERVICE` (appeals courts) and `FJC_JUDGE`, with first initial breaking ties.
- **688 of 703 land (97.9%).** 33 of those differ on first name, and all are nicknames or initials: Oldham "Andy" = Andrew, Rao "Naomi" = Neomi, Wilkinson "J. Harvie" = James.
- **Coverage ends in early 2024.** In JCS by year of commission:

```
commissioned   FJC appeals judges   in JCS
2017                  9                7    (Barrett, Katsas missing)
2021                 11                9    (Pérez, Gelpí: accents break the name match)
2023                 12               10
2024                  5                1
2025                  7                0
```

- The newest matched commission is 2024-01-31. A miss for anyone seated after that means nothing.

**Spin-off lead: senior status now follows the party in power**
The FJC service table has each appeals judge's appointing party, commission date and senior-status date. JCS adds nothing to this lead (see below). It belongs to `FED_FJC_SERVICE`.

- **Test.** For each presidency, take every active appeals judge who was already eligible for senior status on inauguration day (age 65+, age + years of service 80+). Count how many took senior status before the term ended. Split by whether the sitting president is from the appointing president's party.

```
presidency   same party: went / eligible   other party: went / eligible   ratio
Carter           5 / 10   50%                 1 /  4   25%                2.0  (4 judges)
Reagan           9 / 10   90%                 8 / 13   62%                1.5
Bush 41          4 /  8   50%                 9 / 13   69%                0.7
Clinton         10 / 16   62%                 5 /  8   62%                1.0
Bush 43         11 / 16   69%                 3 /  7   43%                1.6
Obama           12 / 25   48%                10 / 30   33%                1.4
Trump 1         16 / 40   40%                 4 / 31   13%                3.1   p=0.016
Biden           28 / 38   74%                 6 / 28   21%                3.4   p=0.00005
```

- **Is it 3-4 circuits?** No.
  - Biden: goers came from 12 circuits. The Ninth had the most (7 of 10). Without the Ninth it's 21/28 (75%) vs 6/25 (24%).
  - Trump 1: goers came from 10 circuits. Without the Sixth (4 of 5) it's 12/35 (34%) vs 4/28 (14%), p=0.087.
- **Dull explanation 1: age.** The same-party pool is a bit older. Under Biden the median age was 73 vs 71.5, and median service 23 years vs 19. **Tested inside age bands. The gap holds:**
  - Biden, age 70-74: 15/18 vs 3/11. Age 75+: 10/13 vs 1/10.
  - Trump 1, age 70-74: 6/11 vs 1/9. Age 75+: 5/14 vs 0/9.
- **The waiters.** These are eligible judges who sat through a president of the other party and were still active when the White House changed hands.
  - 26 Democratic appointees waited out Trump 1. **18 later took senior status, 8 of them in Biden's first 17 months.**
  - 21 Republican appointees waited out Biden. By 2026-06-21, **2 had gone senior** (Ikuta 2025-11-07, Benton 2026-06-21) and **19 are still active**.
    - They include Edith Jones (b. 1949), Jerry Smith (1946), J. Harvie Wilkinson (1944), Paul Niemeyer (1941), James Loken (1940), Alan Lourie (1935) and Pauline Newman (1927).
  - Same first 17 months: Trump 2 waiters 1 of 21 vs Biden waiters 8 of 26 (p=0.03). Benton went one day past the 17 months, and counting him makes it 2 of 21 (p=0.15). **Fragile. Watch it, don't lead with it.**
- **Does JCS add anything?** No. Under Biden, goers and stayers on the same side have the same median score, -0.358. The score can't tell apart judges named by the same president.

**Hit means:** since 2017, appeals judges eligible for senior status time their exit to the party in power at about three times the rate of the other side. Through Obama it was roughly even.
**Miss would have meant:** similar rates on both sides, as in 1989-2000. That's not what the data shows.
**Boring explanations:**
- Strategic retirement is a known idea in political science and has been covered in the press. What's new here is a clean 1977-2026 series, with eligibility and age held fixed, showing the jump.
- Confirmation politics changed. The filibuster for lower courts ended in 2013, and the 2015-16 Senate blocked Obama nominees. Either could explain why Obama-era judges timed exits less. **Not tested.**
- FJC records senior status only when it takes effect. Announced retirements that wait on a successor aren't there yet, so **the Trump 2 window undercounts.**

**Next:** check the uscourts.gov future-vacancies list against the 19 waiters. Build confirmation lag per seat from `FED_FJC_SERVICE` (senior-status date to the successor's commission date).

**Traps**
- ⚠ JCS is a president or senator score, not a judge's voting record. 314 of 703 judges share a score with 4+ others.
- ⚠ The table stops at early-2024 appointments: 1 of 5 from 2024, 0 of 7 from 2025.
- ⚠ The 5th/11th split judges are listed twice. Dedupe by name before counting judges.
- Accented names (Pérez, Gelpí) fail a plain name join to FJC.

## 2. FED_CONGRESS_COMMITTEE_MEMBERSHIP: probed

The Senate-trades-vs-committee angle has been run twice. fable_hunt B9 covered 2012-20, and Perdue drives it. deep2/deep-6 covered 2021-26 and came back dead. I didn't rerun it.

**Checked: what a "snapshot" is**
- Each Congress has exactly one snapshot, one SHA and one date:

```
113  2014-11-13     117  2022-11-29
114  2016-12-17     118  2024-12-17
115  2019-01-02     119  2026-09-02  (mid-Congress)
116  2020-12-07
```

- **Roster completeness.** Members whose last term ended partway through a Congress, from `FED_CONGRESS_LEGISLATORS`:
  - 117th: 19 left early, **0 on the roster**
  - 118th: 18 left early, **0 on the roster** (Santos, McCarthy, Feinstein, Menendez, Gaetz, Gallagher, Sheila Jackson Lee)
  - 119th: 15 left early, **0 on the roster** (Rubio, Vance, Waltz, Greene)
- So the roster shows who sat on committees on one day. It is not everyone who sat on them during that Congress. Match a trade or vote dated before a mid-Congress change and it gets the wrong committee, or none.

**Checked: concentration (the glance's "top holders")**
- The glance's top IDs (R000122 Reed, B001236 Boozman, S001181 Shaheen) are Senate members swollen by ex officio seats.
  - In the 119th, Reed has 20 rows. **8 are ex officio.** Wicker: 8 of 19. Shaheen: 7 of 22.
  - Deb Fischer has 22 real seats and no ex officio rows.

**Checked: does a gavel bring PAC money? (join to `MEMBER_PAC_MONEY` on BIOGUIDE)**
- The PAC table has only the 2024 and 2026 cycles, and 2026 is partial. So the test is each member's 2026 PAC money divided by their 2024 PAC money, 118th House vs 119th House.
- **Median ratio:**
  - Gained a full-committee chair: 0.756 (n=8)
  - Other majority members: 0.685 (n=154)
  - Kept a chair: 0.596 (n=15)
  - Lost a chair: 0.528 (n=4)
- Brett Guthrie (Energy and Commerce) is the only new chair whose money rose ($2.49M to $2.81M). **No gavel bump beyond noise.**

**Checked: "cash committee" freshmen (peer = same-Congress freshmen, same side, same outside-money targeting)**
- Freshman = first appears on any roster in that Congress. 92 of 92 freshmen from the 118th land in the PAC table, and 72 of 72 from the 119th.
- 118th majority freshmen, 2024 PAC money, median:
  - Financial Services: $1.12M (n=6)
  - Ways and Means / Energy and Commerce / Appropriations: $1.51M (n=4)
  - Everyone else: $596K (n=38)
- Holding targeting fixed ($1M+ in outside money):
  - Targeted: Financial Services $2.28M (n=3) vs others $1.52M (n=9)
  - Not targeted: $902K (n=3) vs $453K (n=29)
- The direction is real, but the cells hold 3-6 members, and the dull explanation is the design itself. Leadership puts vulnerable freshmen on money committees so they can raise money. **Known, thin, not a lead.**

**Hit means:** committee seats move PAC money.
**Miss means:** they don't, at least within 20 months. The gavel test missed. The freshman premium is too thin to stand alone.

**Traps**
- ⚠ **Departed members vanish.** 0 of 52 mid-Congress leavers appear. The 119th is a September 2026 snapshot, not a full Congress.
- ⚠ Ex officio seats sit in the same rows as real seats (Senate: 378 majority and 382 minority subcommittee rows). Seat counts need `TITLE <> 'Ex Officio'`.
- Chair titles are spelled several ways: Chair, Chairman, Chairwoman, Cochairman, Co-Chairman, Chairperson. House subcommittees used "Chairman" in 3 Congresses. Filter with `ILIKE 'chair%'` and `NOT ILIKE '%vice%'`.
- 4 Senate majority-side subcommittee rows carry "Ranking Member".
- `PARTY` means majority or minority, not the member's party. The catalog already says so.
- ⚠ In the joined table, `MEMBER_PAC_MONEY` 2024 has a **blank-BIOGUIDE row: $29.26M in PAC money, 2 donors, $237.6M outside-for.** That's 5.3% of the cycle's PAC total. Drop it before ranking.

## 3. FED_MEDSL_HOUSE_RETURNS: probed

**Checked: shape**
- **22 elections, 1976-2018. Nothing after 2018.** All 50 states every year. VOTE_MODE is "total" on every row.
- So g13's Georgia no-choice lead can't be brought up to date from here. `WHO_WON` stops at 2018 too, so the warehouse has **no House results for 2020-2024.**

**Checked: time (competitiveness, 1976-2018)**
- Method: regular general elections only. Each candidate's votes are summed across party lines. Blank and scattering lines are dropped.

```
year   races   no real opponent (<5%)   won by 40+ pts   won by <5 pts
1976    435           38                     171              36
1988    435           66                     232              16
1992    435           18                     112              44
2004    435           38                     161              10
2010    435           13                     127              39
2018    435           19                     122              48
```

- 2018 has the most sub-5-point races in the series, and the median margin is at its low (0.261, tied with 1992).
  - Dull explanation: 2018 was a wave year. It's a known story, and the series can't say whether it lasted.

**Checked: peer (turnout inside a state)**
- Contested races (runner-up 25%+), each district's total votes against the state's median contested district in the same year. Only states with 5+ contested districts.
- 0 to 4 districts a year fall under half their state's median.
  - 2018's lowest: CA-21, 227,232 votes vs the California median of 463,864 (0.49).
  - Next: TX-34, 143,068 vs Texas's 249,822 (0.57).
- The dull explanation is districts drawn on total population, not voters (non-citizens, children). **Not tested.** There's no citizen-voting-age table here. Moderate and well known.

**Hit means:** a House race stands far outside its peers.
**Miss means:** almost nothing does. 2018's worst is 0.49 of its state median.
- Two district-years sit near a quarter of their state median: one in 1992 (0.238) and one in 2008 (0.234). **Not examined.** Budget ran out. A December runoff-style general, like Louisiana's, is the likely dull reason.

**Traps (for the ledger)**
- ⚠ **Ends in 2018.** Any "latest House result" query returns 2018.
- ⚠ **Fusion lines.** A candidate on several party lines (NY, CT) gets one row per line: 44-109 extra rows a year. Sum by candidate before ranking or computing margins.
- ⚠ "Blank Vote/Scattering" and void or over/under rows pose as candidates (the glance's "top candidate", 10.4M votes). They appear in some years only: 0 rows in 1980, 1982, 1984 and 2018, and 97 in 2016.
- In 540 of 9,553 races (5.7%), the named candidates add up to more than 1% below TOTAL_VOTES, and never above it. Vote shares use different bases in different states.
- 2-21 races a year have TOTAL_VOTES of 0 or 1: filler for unopposed seats.
- No 1984 row is flagged IS_WRITEIN. Other years have 58-151 write-in rows.
- 1996 has 422 regular races and 2006 has 431. The missing 13 and 4 seats are in rows flagged special (60 rows in 1996, 30 in 2006), and 1996 also carries a "pri" stage. This matches the Texas court-redrawn districts g13 found missing in WHO_WON. **State not checked here.** A "regular elections only" filter drops them.
- 2006 has a blank STAGE value.

## 4. CA_LOBBY_EMP_LOBBYIST: dead

**Checked**
- 1,500 rows: 286 in the 1995 session, 322 in 1997, 430 in 1999, 462 in 2001. Each lobbyist ID appears once per session. There's one load run.
- **Join to `CA_LOBBY_EMPLOYER` (EMPLOYER_ID + SESSION_ID).** Only 1999 lands: 206 of 252 employers, $54.0M. 1995, 1997 and 2001 land 0, because the employer table holds only the 1999 session.
- Spend per in-house lobbyist, 1999: Western States Petroleum Association had $3.8M and 1 in-house lobbyist listed. But the spend includes payments to outside firms, so this isn't a real denominator. **Not a finding.**
- ⚠ **EMPLOYER_NAME holds the lobbyist's surname on some rows**: 35 of 430 in 1999 and **134 of 462 in 2001**. That's why employer ID 1146926 carries "SEMPRA ENERGY…", SWARTZ, RICHBURG HOWELL, KATZ, MURRAY and PECK.
  - Taking MAX(EMPLOYER_NAME) for the employer with the most lobbyists in 1995 and 1997 returns a surname, GLASER.
  - Use EMPLOYER_ID, and take the name from `CA_LOBBY_EMPLOYER`.

**Hit would have meant:** a way to rank who staffs up in-house.
**Miss means:** it's a 25-year-old roster with no money of its own and a broken name field. Usable only for 1995-2001 history.

## 5. CA_LOBBY_FIRM_LOBBYIST: dead

**Checked**
- 577 rows, all from the 2001 session. 340 firms, one lobbyist ID per row.
- **Join to `CA_LOBBY_FIRM` (FIRM_ID + SESSION_ID):** 254 of 340 firms land, $88.4M.
  - The highest spend per lobbyist was about $0.5-0.8M: Gonsalves & Son, Hyde Miller Owen & Trost, Richard Robinson & Associates.
- **Revolving door, in-house to firm.** 18 lobbyist IDs were in-house in 1995-1999 and at a firm by 2001.
  - Roxanne Gould-Arreguin: Bank of America → Wilke, Fleury, Hoffelt, Gould & Birney
  - Thomas Bone and Donna Kaylor: California Healthcare Association → Platinum Advisors
  - Gavin McHugh: Texaco, then CMTA → his own firm
- 77 of 577 rows are firms named after the lobbyist ("MC FADDEN, LESLIE A."). Those are sole proprietors, not an error.

**Hit would have meant:** a live revolving-door map.
**Miss means:** a single 2001 session. 18 moves from 25 years ago aren't news.
