Companion volume — chart vocabulary, tailored to the tokens

108 named chart types, sorted into 10 families, each built into a Ripple brick.

The layout-systems survey covered how a PAGE gets composed. This covers something narrower and more concrete: the actual visual vocabulary for turning one number, one rate, one network, one document into a mark on the page — using the real chart types data journalism and dataviz practice already invented, translated into Ripple's actual tokens (the blueprint palette, the drafting-tick leader lines, the dashed hairlines, the one-reserved-accent discipline) instead of a generic chart library's defaults.

Three families were already solved by the hand-built page (magnitude, part-to-whole, before/after) and aren't repeated here. 01

Ranking / Comparison Across Many Entities

Given many entities measured the same way, who's the worst, who's the best, and how does each one sit against the rest of the field — as a single snapshot, as a shift between two points, or as a moving position over time. This is the family that turns "we looked at everyone, the same way" into something a reader can actually see.▸
Build first

The ordered bar chart and the dumbbell/connected dot plot. The bar chart is the highest-odds-of-use chart in the entire family — almost every future investigation will need a "who's worst, ranked" moment, and it reuses the page's existing row/hairline/leader-line grammar almost without modification. The dumbbell plot comes second because Ripple's stories are fundamentally about CHANGE, not just standing — "which of these 50 agencies got worse the most" is the multi-entity version of the single before/after moment the site has already proven works. The leaderboard-with-embedded-bars is a strong third if Catalog/Library utility work is next on the roadmap, since it's the natural "look up your own entity" shape for that surface.

Construction rules & brief fit for this family

Cross-cutting construction rules

Every ranking brick needs its own explicit demotion mechanism for the N-1 entities that aren't the point: the reserved accent belongs to ONE entity, gap, or crossing-line at a time (the #1 bar, the biggest dumbbell gap, the one slope that crosses everyone else), never to "the ranking" as a whole — everything else stays neutral var(--bp)/var(--muted)/var(--dim). Sort order and relative position/length/icon-count communicate magnitude before any printed number does — value labels are secondary mono annotations, not the headline. This family is inherently denser than magnitude/part-to-whole/before-after (real N is 20, 50, sometimes thousands), so each brick needs its own built-in rest move — collapsing a long tail, capping full-color labels to a handful, or pairing a loud grid with a single quiet caption line right after it. Every brick visibly borrows the page's existing grammar: 1px dashed var(--hair) row dividers doubling as scale lines, drafting-tick leader lines into mono margin captions, tabular-nums JetBrains Mono for every printed value, tilted "Fig. 0X" captions, and fixed-radius dots (never the sqrt-area-true circles reserved for part-to-whole).

Fit against the music brief

Fits unusually well because most bricks are re-shapes of vocabulary Ripple already proved, not new inventions: the dumbbell plot is the before/after brick stretched to many rows; the slopegraph is a smaller cousin of the page's own dotted spine device; the small-multiples grid generalizes the existing tick-wall; the leaderboard table reuses tabular-nums and hairline dividers wholesale. The two genuinely weak fits — radar and parallel coordinates — are weak for the same reason: they ask the reader to hold multiple competing shapes or brush an axis interactively, fighting both the one-sun rule and the reader-not-analyst posture of a published page.

Ordered (sorted) bar charthigh

ShowsHow much each of N entities has, arranged so magnitude and rank read in one sweep — the default answer to "who's biggest/worst, and by how much."

ExemplarProPublica's "Dollars for Docs" — a sortable horizontal bar ranking of pharmaceutical/device companies by total payments made to individual doctors.

Ripple brickRows of horizontal bars, one per entity, stacked with 1px dashed var(--hair) dividers between rows. Mono entity label right-aligned; the bar is an SVG rect or CSS div, width = value/max, ~22px tall; value in mono, tabular-nums, at the bar's end. Everything renders in neutral var(--bp) EXCEPT the #1 bar, which alone takes the investigation's one reserved accent — that bar is the scene's sun. A drafting-tick leader line runs off it into a mono margin annotation. For real N (20+), collapse everything past the top 8-10 into a dimmed "long tail" cluster with one caption line ("+42 more, avg 3% of #1") instead of 50 equally loud bars.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS. No library needed at any realistic N.

Lollipop chartmedium

ShowsThe same ranking as a bar chart, but a thin stem + a dot at the value instead of a filled bar — keeps the eye on the endpoint number, the standard declutter move once a bar list gets long.

ExemplarDatawrapper's own chart-type guidance names it explicitly as the bar-chart alternative for medium-to-long ranked lists; a recurring FiveThirtyEight convention.

Ripple brickA 1px line in var(--hair)/var(--dim) from the axis to the value, capped by a small circle (r=4-5px) — a FIXED radius, deliberately not the sqrt-area-true scaling reserved for part-to-whole circles. Only #1, or an entity crossing a named threshold, gets a filled, larger (r=6) dot in the reserved accent; everyone else stays hollow var(--muted). Best used as the deliberately quiet second scene right after a loud ordered-bar scene of the same data.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS, trivial.

Cleveland dot plothigh

ShowsOne value per entity plotted as a position along a shared scale (not a length) — the most perceptually accurate way to compare rates, especially when values don't meaningfully start at zero.

ExemplarPew Research Center's signature chart form for cross-country/cross-group comparisons; the Urban Institute's Data Visualization Style Guide explicitly recommends it over bar charts for this use.

Ripple brickSame row layout as the bar chart, but the full-row-width dashed var(--hair) divider doubles as the literal scale line the dot sits on. Mono entity label left; filled circle (r=5, var(--bp)) at its value. The outlier(s) that are the actual story get recolored into the reserved accent with a drafting-tick leader to a margin caption. Never implies a zero baseline the way a bar's length does.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS, identical cost to the bar chart.

Dumbbell / connected dot plot (range plot)high

ShowsTwo values per entity — before/after, min/max, this-entity-vs-benchmark — as two dots joined by a line, down a ranked list of many entities. The many-entity sibling of the already-solved single before/after brick.

ExemplarThe Urban Institute's style guide names it a signature form for policy-gap stories; The Economist and NYT have both run dumbbell charts for state-by-state or country-by-country gap stories.

Ripple brickDirectly extends the existing repair-row pattern, reshaped from one hero pair into N stacked rows. Old value = hollow/dim circle (same tone as the struck "0%"); new value = filled circle in the reserved accent, joined by a plain connecting stem (the full arrowhead stays reserved for the single hero moment). Rows sorted by the SIZE of the gap — the only honest sort axis.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS — two circles plus one line per row, no library needed even at 50+ rows.

Slopegraphhigh

ShowsRank or value at exactly two points for many entities at once, each entity as one line running left to right — crossing lines are the story (who leapfrogged whom).

ExemplarInvented and named by Edward Tufte (cancer survival rates by country); a recurring form in NYT and Reuters Graphics demographic/election reporting.

Ripple brickTwo mono-labeled columns with N thin lines between them. The bulk render in var(--hair)/var(--dim) at 1px as quiet texture; the one or two lines that are the actual finding break into the reserved accent at 2px, labeled only there — the crossing pattern should be felt as a shape first, read as a name second. Sits close to the page's existing dotted-spine device at smaller scale.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS, hand-rollable at 50-100 entities.

Bump chart (rank flow over time)medium

ShowsHow each entity's RANK (not raw value) moves across three or more periods — a slopegraph generalized to many x-positions.

ExemplarFT's recurring Formula 1 championship-standings bump chart; a documented staple for sports-league table trackers (Datawrapper teaching example).

Ripple brickThe slopegraph vocabulary extended to K columns, y-position always RANK not raw value, across K dashed guide-lines. Hard-cap full-color labeled lines to the handful the story is about; drop everyone else to opacity:.25 backdrop texture — "one sun, everyone else recedes" in its purest form.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS up to ~15-20 lines; past that a library (D3) earns its keep for collision-avoided labels.

Radar / spider chartlow

ShowsProfiles a single entity across many attributes at once as spokes around a circle, so the resulting polygon shape becomes the entity's "signature."

ExemplarFIFA/EA Sports player-attribute radars are the most globally recognized use; no honest non-sports/gaming investigative-journalism exemplar found.

Ripple brickIf ever used: dashed rings at 25/50/75/100%, one translucent filled polygon per entity. Flag clearly: overlaying two entities creates two competing polygons — two suns fighting one scene — and polygon AREA reads as magnitude even though it's an artifact of axis order, the opposite of the area-true honesty the circles already commit to.

BuildEasy to build (simple trig) — the real difficulty is design justification, not code.

Parallel coordinates plotlow

ShowsMany entities as one line each, crossing K parallel vertical axes — useful for spotting clusters/outliers across many dimensions at once. An analyst's exploration tool.

ExemplarAlfred Inselberg's formalization; real editorial use is genuinely rare — closer to Tableau/Plotly analyst dashboards than finished journalism.

Ripple brickIf ever used: K vertical axes, one polyline per entity in var(--dim) low-opacity background, reserved accent held for the 1-3 entities that are the story.

BuildStatic rendering is plain SVG, but the real reason to reach for this chart is interactive axis-brushing, which genuinely wants D3/Observable Plot — treat any real build as a tooling job, not a static brick.

Small-multiples ranked grid (trellis/lattice)high

ShowsN entities each get an identical miniature chart, arranged in a grid sorted by rank — lets a reader scan the WHOLE ranked field at once.

ExemplarThe Pudding's grid-based visual essays; NYT's COVID-19 state-by-state tracker (a grid of 50 state mini-charts, sorted by case rate).

Ripple brickA CSS grid of small cells, each a miniature bar or lollipop, in reading order by rank — generalizes the page's existing tickwall grid into differentiated per-entity minis. Reserved accent highlights only the cell(s) the story is about. High-value specifically because it shows ALL entities at once, matching the "census, not subpoena" instinct.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS, the same primitive as the existing tick wall.

Leaderboard / ranked table with embedded barshigh

ShowsA numbered list where each row carries both a rank AND a small in-line bar — most useful when the reader wants to look up ONE specific entity.

ExemplarProPublica's "Nonprofit Explorer" and ICIJ's Offshore Leaks searchable ranked databases.

Ripple brickMono-numbered list (tabular-nums), entity name in Work Sans, embedded bar sized to a SHARED max across the whole table so bars stay comparable row to row. The #1 row — or, if interactive, the row matching the reader's own search — is the sun.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS for the row treatment; sortable/searchable needs a small amount of vanilla JS, matching how the page already handles the tick-wall and spine.

Unit chart / ISOTYPE pictogram rankingmedium

ShowsRanks entities by representing each one's count as a row of repeated small identical icons rather than a smooth bar — makes "how many actual things or people" concrete.

ExemplarOtto Neurath and Gerd Arntz's original ISOTYPE work — the literal foundational reference; modern NPR/Vox "each icon = 1,000 people" pictogram rankings.

Ripple brickThe ranking-scale extension of the already-solved icon-array brick — same glyph-repeat + crop-remainder mechanic, stacked as N rows sorted by icon count. A strong "scale felt before read" device, but gets heavy past 8-12 rows.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS, same construction as the already-solved icon-array brick.

02

Time / Trend

Not "how big is it" or "how does it split" — "is it getting better or worse, and exactly when did that change." Ripple's mission-critical version: did the harm get worse after a rule changed, a regulator stopped enforcing, or an exclusion period lapsed — and can I point at the exact date it happened.▸
Build first

The plain line chart (with the drafting-tick inflection-point leader and self-labeling terminus) and the sparkline. The line chart earns its place because almost every other entry here — step, cumulative, area, rebased — is the SAME scale math and path-builder with one data transform bolted on, so building it well effectively buys 4-5 chart types for the price of one. The sparkline is the cheapest possible build in the entire family and plugs directly into the ranking-table/leaderboard instinct, instantly adding a trend dimension to every future "many entities, one table" page.

Construction rules & brief fit for this family

Cross-cutting construction rules

Every trend brick shares one job the three solved families don't have: pointing at a SPECIFIC MOMENT, not just plotting a shape — the drafting-tick leader plus tilted "Fig. 0X" caption is the mechanism that turns "here's a line" into "here's the year the rule changed." One reserved accent, always var(--rust) for the story series, everything else recedes to var(--ink-soft)/var(--muted)/var(--dim). One dominant shape per scene. No boxed plot area, no dense gridlines — a single dashed var(--hair) baseline does the axis's job. Self-labeling over legends: lines get their value/name at their own terminus. Y-axes stay honest and zero-baselined by default (never a silent truncated axis) — the rebased chart's "start=100" line is the one deliberate, always-disclosed exception. Density needs rest: a dense multi-series scene should be followed by a quiet single-number moment. Tabular-nums throughout.

Fit against the music brief

Arguably the most load-bearing unsolved family relative to Ripple's actual mission — "find the systemic pattern first" is inherently a claim about change over time. A trend line's shape is the purest available instance of "scale felt before read" (a steepening line or a filling area IS the finding, before any number is read); the one-sun rule maps onto "one rust line among many neutral peer lines"; the drafting-tick-leader convention is really the visual form of "point at the exact moment power failed someone." Small multiples, slope charts, and bump charts specifically show one flagged entity's line standing out against the full, honest field of every other entity measured the same way — not cherry-picked, just the loudest line in a fair lineup.

Line chart (single- and multi-series, with inflection annotation)high

ShowsHow one or several measured quantities move over continuous time — the default "is it getting better or worse, and exactly when did it turn" chart.

ExemplarFT's John Burn-Murdoch COVID "trajectory" line charts; the econ-data line charts on FRED that most econ journalism traces back to.

Ripple brickHand-rolled SVG polyline, one dashed var(--hair) baseline (no full grid). X-axis date ticks sparse (start/end/one inflection). The story series renders 2.5px in var(--rust); every comparison series renders 1px var(--ink-soft)/var(--muted) at 40-60% opacity — no legend box, each line self-labels at its terminal point. The inflection point gets a drafting-tick leader to a tilted "Fig. 0X" caption — this leader mechanism is the one piece of apparatus every other chart in the family reuses.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS. Only reach for a library for true pan/zoom/brush or 50+ simultaneous series.

Step chart (step-after line)high

ShowsA quantity that jumps at discrete moments rather than drifting continuously — a rate or policy level that holds flat then jumps, because nothing happened between the jumps.

ExemplarFRED's effective federal-funds-rate chart; the same convention in WSJ/Bloomberg Fed-rate-history graphics and minimum-wage-by-state explainers (Vox, NYT).

Ripple brickSame scale math as the line chart, but built with explicit horizontal-then-vertical step-after segments — a genuinely different path-builder. Line in var(--rust) when the threshold IS the story; every jump gets its own vertical drafting-tick plus a date label, since the jump is the whole finding.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS, same scale code as the line chart, different path builder.

Cumulative total / running-sum charthigh

ShowsNot "how much per period" but "how much total, ever, by this point" — turns a noisy per-period series into a smooth, monotonic account of accumulated harm.

ExemplarNYT's COVID tracker's daily-new vs. cumulative-total toggle; Reuters' running COVID death-toll graphics.

Ripple brickSame filled-area construction as the area chart, computed on a running-sum transform. Because it never goes down, it's a strong standalone "sun" shape for a hero scene with one terminal numeral. Pair with a small adjacent sparkline of the raw per-period rate, since a cumulative curve can keep climbing even while the rate is falling — an honesty risk worth flagging.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS, trivial running-sum preprocessing.

Area chart (filled single-series)high

ShowsThe line chart's magnitude-emphasized cousin — the filled area under the curve makes the "weight" of the number over time felt, not just its direction.

ExemplarNYT federal budget/deficit area charts; FRED/Calculated Risk recession-shaded GDP area charts.

Ripple brickSVG path closed to baseline, gradient fill from var(--rust) at ~35% opacity to transparent, solid 2px stroke on top edge. This fill IS the scene's one sun — never add a second filled series alongside it; a context series must render as an unfilled thin line only.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS, same scale math as the line chart plus a closed path.

Rebased / indexed line chartmedium

ShowsSeveral series on wildly different scales, redrawn as "% change from a shared start point" so their trajectories become directly comparable.

ExemplarThe Economist's constant "indexed, [year]=100" line charts; FT's rebased stock-performance charts.

Ripple brickIdentical construction to the multi-line chart, preprocessed by dividing every series by its own first value ×100. A single mono "100" reference line at the shared start is the gut-check line. Needs an honesty caption every time, since rebasing can mislead when a small denominator swings wildly.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS, same code as the line chart with one preprocessing step.

Stacked area chartmedium

ShowsHow the mix of categories inside a total shifts year over year — not just the total's size, but which slice is growing or shrinking.

ExemplarPew Research's stacked-area charts of U.S. religious composition, 2007-2021; NYT's industry-composition-of-immigration stacked areas.

Ripple brickStacked SVG paths from cumulative sums. All bands neutral (sage/teal/bp/muted/dim) except the one band under investigation, which gets var(--rust), a heavier top stroke, and an end-label. Pin the story band against the zero baseline so its true shape reads without being squeezed by neighbors' wobble.

BuildPlain SVG, no library at typical category counts, but stacking arithmetic needs care.

Waterfall chart (bridge chart)medium

ShowsHow a total walked from a starting value to an ending value through a sequence of positive/negative contributions — which piece moved it.

ExemplarA McKinsey-popularized business-reporting staple; WSJ and Bloomberg break a company's or agency's year-over-year budget change down piece by piece.

Ripple brickSequential floating rects, each running from the prior total to the new total, connected by dashed step-connectors with a mono delta callout. Positive/improving in var(--sage), negative/harm in var(--rust). Start and end totals render as solid full-weight anchor bars, heavier than any of the small deltas.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS, running-sum arithmetic.

Sparklinehigh

ShowsA tiny, axis-less inline trend line meant to sit in text or a table cell — "what's the shape of this number's history," no precision claimed.

ExemplarEdward Tufte's original sparkline concept; FT and WSJ market-data tables use them routinely; Datawrapper formalizes the same as a newsroom-table chart type.

Ripple brickOne small inline polyline (~80x24px), no axes, var(--ink-soft) stroke. In a ranked list of many entities, the story entity's sparkline recolors var(--rust) so it pops — the one-sun rule applied at list scale. A small end-dot plus one mono numeral (current value or delta) keeps it from breaking "scale felt before read."

BuildThe cheapest chart in the entire family — a good first proof the brick approach works.

Small multiples of line/area charts (trellis panel)high

ShowsThe same trend metric repeated in many small, identically-scaled panels, one per entity — trades one big precise chart for many small comparable ones.

ExemplarNYT's small-multiples grid of every state's COVID case curve; The Economist runs small-multiples line grids constantly for country comparisons; Tufte's "small multiple" concept.

Ripple brickA CSS grid of identical tiny panels (the sparkline brick tiled), all sharing one fixed scale — a hard requirement, since mismatched per-panel scales is the #1 way this lies. Panels default var(--ink-soft); the panel(s) matching the subject get a rust surface-tint plus stroke, jumping out before a label is read.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS, no library even at 50-100 panels; just enforce the shared-scale rule.

Slope chart (multi-entity, two-time-point)high

ShowsFor many entities at once, how each one's value moved between exactly two moments — ranks and crossovers become visible where paired bar charts would hide them.

ExemplarTufte's original slopegraph (cancer survival by country); WSJ and Datawrapper's "Slope chart" preset for before/after policy comparisons.

Ripple brickThe multi-entity cousin of the solved before/after brick — its exact grammar repeated as N short parallel segments. Most segments thin var(--ink-soft)/var(--dim); only the investigated entity full-weight var(--rust). Crossing lines are left uncleaned — an entity moving "the wrong way" is often the finding itself.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS, cheap even at 50-100 entities — mostly the existing before/after brick multiplied.

Bump chart (rank-over-time)medium

ShowsHow many entities' ordinal rank changes across a small number of time steps — built for "who overtook whom, and when," not "by how much."

ExemplarFiveThirtyEight's and The Pudding's sports power-ranking bump charts across a season; Datawrapper's "Bump chart" preset.

Ripple brickSame crossing-line construction as the slope chart, but fixed evenly-spaced rank-slots instead of value-scaled positions, and 3+ time-step columns. Only legible with a curated small N — the full 50+ entity census as a bump chart is noise, and needs a "top 10 plus the one we're chasing" cut.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS for under ~15-20 lines; past that, hand-computing a clean crossing layout hurts and a library earns its keep.

Connected scatterplotmedium

ShowsTwo variables plotted against each other with a line connecting the points in chronological order — reveals a trajectory or reversal neither a plain scatter nor a plain line chart shows alone.

ExemplarHannah Fairfield's NYT graphic "Driving Shifts Into Reverse" (2010, gas prices vs. miles driven) — the canonical, most-cited instance.

Ripple brickSVG path connecting points in time order, structural var(--bp) stroke since the two axes do the storytelling. The most recent point and/or a sharp reversal gets promoted to var(--rust) with a year label — "the year it reversed" is usually the actual finding. An occasional feature chart, not a workhorse — genuinely harder for a cold reader to parse.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS, same primitives as the line chart.

Calendar heatmaphigh

ShowsHow a daily count distributes across the calendar — day-of-week clusters, seasonality, or the one specific date something spiked.

ExemplarThe GitHub contributions graph is the most widely recognized instance generally; Reuters Graphics' calendar-heatmap incident-date treatments; Datawrapper's built-in "Calendar chart" type.

Ripple brickGrid of small squares (7 cols × ~52 rows), reusing the exact tickwall grid pattern. Empty days reuse the hollow-dashed "gone" tick state. Populated days scale on a single-hue ramp up to var(--rust) — deliberately not green, since sage already means "resolved/healthy" and this is almost always tracking something you don't want healthy.

BuildPlain CSS grid + inline SVG rects, no library even at multi-year scale.

Interval / duration timeline (Gantt-style)high

ShowsNot a value changing over time, but WHEN something started and ended and how many such intervals overlap — durations, tenures, exclusion periods, laid end to end.

ExemplarProPublica and Reuters have both used Gantt-style timelines for "how long each disciplinary case stayed open" or overlapping-tenure stories.

Ripple brickHorizontal rects, one row per entity/case, left edge = start, width = duration, on a shared date axis. A case still open gets a soft gradient fade to transparent instead of a hard stop — the drawing itself tells you it's unresolved before any label does. Rows sorted by duration (longest first) so the sorted shape reads as a rough distribution before any single row is read.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS, no library even at a few hundred rows.

Streamgraph (ThemeRiver)low

ShowsThe same composition-over-time data as a stacked area chart, centered/wiggled around a floating baseline instead of a flat zero-axis — optimized for organic swell/shrink shape over precise per-band values.

ExemplarLee Byron and Martin Wattenberg's NYT interactive "The Ebb and Flow of Movies" (2008) — the piece that popularized the form.

Ripple brickSame stacked-path arithmetic, wiggle-offset baseline. Keep restrained versus the NYT rainbow original — neutral bands at soft opacity, one rust band for the story category, zero gridlines. Use sparingly, for a single "shape of an era's caseload mix" scene at most, never as primary evidence.

BuildPlain SVG for a handful of bands; the wiggle-baseline math gets hard past that — a real case where Observable Plot's stack-offset helpers save time.

Horizon chartlow

ShowsCrams many time series into a small vertical footprint by folding amplitude into stacked color bands instead of chart height — trades precision for comparing dozens of series' shape on one screen.

ExemplarStudied in Heer, Kong & Agrawala's "Sizing the Horizon" (CHI 2009); popularized via D3 examples. Production use skews toward ops/monitoring dashboards, not newsroom journalism.

Ripple brickThe one item actively cautioned against building: folding breaks "scale felt before read" (a folded band's true height doesn't mean what it looks like until you've read the fold count), and cramming 20+ series onto one screen fights the one-sun-per-scene rule directly.

BuildConstructible but nontrivial; the honest recommendation is not to build it at all.

Candlestick / OHLC chartlow

ShowsFour numbers at once (open/high/low/close) for a single period — a form built specifically for financial price-and-trading-range data.

ExemplarEvery stock-market chart on Bloomberg, WSJ Markets, or Yahoo Finance.

Ripple brickSmall rects with wicks, up-days sage, down-days rust — included here so finance-shaped chart types aren't silently skipped, not because there's a natural Ripple use today.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS if genuinely needed, but not worth pre-building against a hypothetical story.

03

Distribution

Given one measurable quantity across a population of things — dollars fined, days to resolution, violation counts — how are the values actually spread out? What's typical, how fat is the outlier tail, is the population secretly two populations, and — the sharpest, most investigative version — how much of the total is concentrated in a small handful of entities versus shared out evenly. This is the family that turns "here's one bad case" into "here's the shape of the whole thing."▸
Build first

The histogram. It's the cheapest possible extension of what already exists (the tickwall grid and drafting-tick leader-line vocabulary are already built), it needs zero new math beyond a GROUP BY, and it's the single most reusable brick across almost any future investigation. Second, the ECDF, with the Lorenz curve as a near-free variant on the same sort-and-cumulate code. This pairing earns its priority on mission fit as much as build cost: "X% of entities account for Y% of the harm" is close to Ripple's most repeated sentence, and both reuse the before/after family's already-solved "one gold accent, one leader line" discipline. Box plot and strip plot are close seconds — cheap, hand-rollable, and cover "compare groups" and "every case is a real receipt."

Construction rules & brief fit for this family

Cross-cutting construction rules

Every brick shares one build discipline: precompute everything (bin counts, KDE values, quartiles, collision layouts, cumulative percentages) ahead of publish, then ship the page as plain, inert SVG. The scene's sun is always the shape of the spread itself (a bar silhouette, a curve, a swarm outline, a box) — never a floating numeral; numerals are always anchored to a specific mark via a drafting-tick leader line. Exactly ONE accent color is reserved per scene, almost always var(--rust) for the flagged group/bin/case or var(--gold) for a single measured line/threshold — every other bin, bar, dot, or lane stays neutral. This is the family most naturally at risk of violating "density needs rest" — several bricks (ridgeline, raincloud, beeswarm at high N) are inherently busy, so each needs a genuinely quiet scene immediately before or after it.

Fit against the music brief

The family most in tension with the brief's spare, one-sun-per-scene discipline, because by nature it wants to show MANY things at once. That tension is why this family needs the strictest color discipline of all ten. Done with restraint, it's one of the best matches for Ripple's actual voice — it can hold the entire honest population AND one individually-named, accent-colored case in the same auditable scene, the literal picture of "the case serves the map, never the other way around." The concentration-shaped bricks (ECDF, Lorenz curve, Pareto chart) fit especially tightly — they state "who holds a disproportionate share of the harm," close to a direct visual restatement of "recon on power."

Histogramhigh

ShowsBins a single numeric variable into equal-width intervals and counts how many cases fall in each — reveals shape (skew, modality), the typical value, and where the outlier tail begins.

ExemplarFiveThirtyEight's presidential-forecast pages publish a binned histogram of the ~20,000-40,000 simulated electoral-vote totals under the win-probability needle.

Ripple brickBuilt directly on the tickwall CSS grid, repurposed as bars: a flex row, one div per bin, height set inline proportional to count so scale is felt, never a floating numeral. The one bin that IS the finding gets solid var(--rust) with a top edge — the only accent, the scene's sun. A drafting-tick leader points off it to a Space Grotesk callout numeral, exactly like the hero scene's deaths-circle treatment.

BuildFully hand-rollable — bar heights are a trivial linear scale off pre-computed bin counts. No library at any realistic bin count.

Frequency polygonmedium

ShowsThe same binned counts as a histogram, drawn as a connected line through each bin's midpoint — built so two or more group distributions can share one set of axes without bar-on-bar occlusion.

ExemplarTraces to Karl Pearson's original statistical graphics; still shows up in CDC MMWR "epi curve" overlays and Pew Research's overlaid subgroup comparisons.

Ripple brickSame bin data as the histogram, drawn as a smooth path through midpoints instead of bars. The investigative-subject group gets 2px var(--rust); comparison groups stay var(--dim) with a hairline fill beneath — never two saturated colors competing for the sun.

BuildTrivial once the histogram's bin counts exist — same numbers, drawn as a path.

Density curve (KDE)medium

ShowsA smoothed, continuous version of the histogram that removes bin-width arbitrariness and makes modality unmistakable — one hump versus two humps reads instantly as a silhouette.

ExemplarNPR Planet Money's "The Incredible Shrinking American Middle Class" (2015); Our World in Data's global income-distribution work showing the population splitting into distinct humps.

Ripple brickOne smooth filled path, sage fill/stroke for the whole-population curve. If the finding is a SPLIT population, the trough between humps gets a single dashed rust drop-line and a mono callout ("the gap") — the smallest mark on the canvas, but by restraint it becomes the scene's sun.

BuildRendering is plain SVG, but the y-values require a real kernel-density computation ahead of time — budget real precompute time.

Cumulative distribution function / ECDFhigh

ShowsFor every possible value on the x-axis, what share of the population falls at or below it — turns "the top few dominate" into one readable S-curve.

ExemplarOur World in Data's poverty-line charts ("share of the world population living below $X/day"); Pew Research's standard construction for income-threshold shares.

Ripple brickOne path, x = sorted value, y = cumulative % of N. Structural var(--bp) stroke. The one accented element is a single drafting-tick pair in var(--gold) dropped at the exact investigative threshold, with a numeral at the intersection — close to Ripple's signature sentence: "X% of entities accounted for Y% of harm."

BuildFully hand-rollable — sort, compute a running percentage, draw the path. Arguably the cheapest-to-build, highest-payoff brick in the whole family.

Lorenz curvehigh

ShowsA specialized cumulative curve for concentration specifically: cumulative share of the population (x) against cumulative share of the thing measured (y) — bowed away from the diagonal "spread evenly" line by exactly how concentrated it is.

ExemplarMax O. Lorenz (1905); underlies the Gini-coefficient graphics Our World in Data and The Economist reuse constantly for inequality reporting.

Ripple brickSame sort-and-cumulate mechanics as the ECDF, plus a dashed diagonal labeled "if it were spread evenly." The curve bows in var(--rust) — the one chart where rust is earned by the math itself, not a stylistic choice. The sliver area between diagonal and curve fills at low opacity, making the inequality literal instead of a bare coefficient.

BuildPlain SVG, same math as the ECDF plus one static diagonal — no library required.

Box plot (box-and-whisker)high

ShowsThe five-number summary (min, Q1, median, Q3, max, outliers) — the fastest way to compare the SPREAD of several groups on one shared axis.

ExemplarJohn Tukey's 1977 "Exploratory Data Analysis"; The Economist's Graphic Detail column regularly runs box-and-whisker comparisons of country- or state-level distributions.

Ripple brickA horizontal group per entity (reads left-to-right like the page's captions): a thin whisker, a surface-fill box, an ink median tick. Only the ONE flagged group gets its box filled rust with outlier dots as small rust circles — every other group stays neutral, so the eye finds the flagged group first even among ten stacked boxes.

BuildFully hand-rollable — quartiles computed ahead of publish. No library needed even for 10-20 groups.

Violin plotmedium

ShowsA box plot's group-comparison power combined with a density curve's shape detail — mirrors the KDE silhouette left-right so both summary stats AND bimodality are visible.

ExemplarHintze & Nelson, "Violin Plots" (1998); adopted in sports-analytics data journalism and science journalism reporting clinical spreads.

Ripple brickTwo mirrored KDE silhouette paths per group; the flagged group fills rust with a slim vertical box-plot core visible inside — drop the box and it becomes decoration instead of a chart. Sample size N printed below each stem, load-bearing because violin width lies about confidence when N is small.

BuildHand-rollable once KDE values are precomputed per group — same math budget as the density-curve brick, times group count.

Strip plot / dot plot (jittered)high

ShowsEvery individual data point plotted as one mark along a single axis, with small deterministic jitter purely to stop overlap — no binning, no smoothing, the rawest honest view because each mark IS one real case.

ExemplarWilliam S. Cleveland's dot plot work (1985); FiveThirtyEight/The Upshot's default "every team, every player, every state as one dot" whenever N is small enough that a bar would waste named information.

Ripple brickSmall circles, one per case, positioned by real value with a light deterministic jitter (a fixed hash of the entity ID, not random, so the chart is reproducible on rebuild). The exact cases that ARE the story get full-opacity rust with a ring so they pop off the crowd. This is the chart type that most literally embodies "the case serves the map" — every mark is a real, individually-auditable entity.

BuildFully hand-rollable for realistic investigative N (tens to a few hundred). Above a few thousand points, canvas or D3-assisted render becomes the honest choice.

Beeswarm plotmedium

ShowsThe same case-level honesty as a strip plot, but positions are packed by real collision-avoidance so the shape the dots pile into becomes a true density silhouette with zero overlap at any N.

ExemplarThe Economist's Graphic Detail beeswarm comparisons of country-level rates; popularized via Mike Bostock's D3 examples.

Ripple brickVisually near-identical to the strip plot (same dots, same color split, same case-is-a-receipt leader-line callout) — the only difference is real force-directed or collision packing instead of a jitter hash.

BuildThe family's clearest case for an actual library — hand-computing collision layout past ~30-40 points isn't reasonable manually. Use d3-force or Observable Plot's dodge transform once at build time, bake coordinates into static SVG.

Ridgeline plot (joyplot)medium

ShowsStacks many overlapping density curves, one per group, offset and slightly overlapping vertically — built to show how a distribution's SHAPE changes across an ordered sequence, usually time.

ExemplarThe name comes from Joy Division's "Unknown Pleasures" album cover; Spotify's data-visualization blog popularized the journalistic use in 2017.

Ripple brickA vertical stack of density silhouettes, offset by a fixed spacing step, in low-opacity neutrals so they read as calm sediment layers — except the ONE investigative lane, which gets rust fill and sits slightly forward. Visually dense by construction, so give it a full quiet scene with nothing else competing.

BuildSame precompute burden as the density curve, multiplied by lanes — plain SVG paths, no library, but budget real design discipline.

Population pyramid (mirrored histogram)medium

ShowsTwo histograms back-to-back around a shared vertical axis — classically age, with two subgroups mirrored left/right — so relative size and shape can be compared bin-by-bin in one glance.

ExemplarThe standard demographic chart published by the U.S. Census Bureau; used narratively by Pew Research when comparing age structure across groups.

Ripple brickLiterally two histogram bricks sharing one central dashed vertical spine, bars extending left and right. One side stays neutral; the other gets the ONE reserved accent — never both sides colored, which would break the single-sun rule.

BuildFully hand-rollable — two histogram builds mirrored around one axis, no library needed.

Raincloud plotmedium

ShowsA deliberate "show everything, hide nothing" hybrid: a half-density "cloud" above, raw individual points as a jittered "rain" below, and a thin box plot in between — summary shape, summary stats, and every raw case visible at once.

Exemplar"Raincloud plots: a multi-platform tool for robust data visualization" (Micah Allen et al., Wellcome Open Research, 2019); adoption concentrates in open-science/science-journalism contexts.

Ripple brickA direct stack of three already-designed bricks sharing one x-axis: density silhouette on top, box-plot core, strip-plot rain of individual dots below — the three layers share one gap and the same x-scale, nothing has to be re-derived.

BuildNo new construction cost beyond the density, box, and strip bricks once they exist — closer to an editorial layout decision than new engineering.

Pareto charthigh

ShowsRanks discrete categories by frequency (descending bars) and overlays a cumulative-percentage line — answers "which few categories account for most of the total," the visual proof of an 80/20-style concentration among named categories.

ExemplarVilfredo Pareto's original observation, turned into a quality-control chart by Joseph Juran; appears in state auditor and inspector-general reports whenever a piece ranks violation types or complaint categories by frequency.

Ripple brickBars ranked descending, built like the histogram's tickwall bars, with named category labels tilted below. A cumulative line in var(--gold) climbs left to right with dot markers. A dashed guide at 80% lets the eye find where the line crosses it; only the bars at-or-left of that crossing promote from dim to rust — the chart recolors itself exactly at the point the claim is proven.

BuildFully hand-rollable — sort, compute cumulative percentage, draw bars plus one line. No library at any realistic category count.

Q-Q plot (quantile-quantile plot)low

ShowsPlots one distribution's quantiles against another's to test whether two sets of values are shaped the same way — a straight diagonal means yes; bends mean no.

ExemplarWilk & Gnanadesikan (1968) — a statistician's diagnostic tool that essentially never appears in general-audience publication. No real mainstream data-journalism exemplar exists, and none should be invented.

Ripple brickN/A for reader-facing use. If ever needed internally to sanity-check a claim, a plain scatter against a dashed 45-degree reference line — but this belongs in an internal QA notebook, never a published scene. It answers "can I trust my own statistics," not "who got hurt," failing the mission's own test outright.

BuildTrivial if ever needed, but skip entirely — treat as an internal-only QA aid at most.

04

Correlation Between Two or More Variables

Do two (or more) measured things move together across a set of entities — and if that relationship is real, how strong is it, who sits at the extreme, and does the pattern hold or break down for one flagged case? This is the family that turns "X happened AND Y happened" into "X happening predicts Y happening."▸
Build first

The scatter plot, then the quadrant chart. Scatter is the base engine — axis construction, the field/flagged-entity color split, the trend-line leader-line annotation — that bubble chart, connected scatterplot, and quadrant chart all reuse directly, so building it once effectively unlocks three more chart types at low marginal cost. Quadrant chart is second, not fourth, because it's nearly free once scatter exists (two divider lines and four plain-language labels) and converts a correlation into a named, plain-words accusation ("who's getting away with it") — the accountability-journalism framing the mission calls for.

Construction rules & brief fit for this family

Cross-cutting construction rules

Every chart here inherently shows MANY entities or MANY variables at once, which fights directly against "one sun per scene." The fix that applies everywhere: split every population into "the field" (translucent, neutral, never more than ~.55 opacity, no individual labels) and "the flagged one/few" (full-opacity rust, individually labeled with a drafting-tick leader) — almost nothing in this family should render every entity at equal visual weight. Reuse existing primitives rather than inventing new ones: the area-true circle math becomes the bubble-size channel; the tickwall becomes marginal rugs. Axes are always hairlines, never gridlines. Never introduce a second accent color for "positive vs negative correlation" — map relationship STRENGTH to the one reserved accent's intensity, reserve rust only for the specific pair/entity that is the actual finding.

Fit against the music brief

A strong structural fit for Ripple's mechanism-first method — "as inspections dropped, injuries rose" is a correlation chart wearing investigative-journalism language. Honest caveat: only about half this family (scatter, bubble, connected scatterplot, quadrant) is realistically reader-facing; the other half (correlation matrix, SPLOM, parallel coordinates, radar) is an investigator screening tool that belongs in Playground, not on a finished page. Build the reader-facing half first; treat the workbench half as tooling for Chris himself, not content for a page.

Scatter plot (with trend line and/or marginal rugs)high

ShowsPlots every entity as one point at (X,Y) so the reader sees, all at once, whether two measured things move together — and the actual shape of the relationship, not just a coefficient.

ExemplarAnscombe's Quartet (1973) is the field's own classic proof you must plot the points, not just compute a correlation. NYT Upshot's "Some Colleges Have More Students From the Top 1 Percent Than the Bottom 60" (2017) scattered parents'-income percentile against student earnings, college by college.

Ripple brickPoints at var(--bp) ~.55 opacity for the whole unremarkable population — the "planets" half of sun/planets. Exactly one (or a small named few) flagged entity gets a larger rust-filled point — the sun. An optional trend line is a single thin dashed line (never a thick regression band), annotated with a drafting-tick leader to a mono caption ("as inspections drop, injuries climb"). Marginal rugs reuse the existing tickwall vocabulary instead of a histogram.

BuildPlain hand-rolled SVG/CSS, trivial at hundreds-to-low-thousands of entities. Above a few thousand raw points, canvas/WebGL or a hexbin density chart is more honest than raw SVG nodes.

Bubble charthigh

ShowsA scatter plot with a third quantity riding on point size — shows a relationship while also showing which entities matter most by scale.

ExemplarHans Rosling / Gapminder's "Wealth & Health of Nations" — income vs. life expectancy, bubble size = population, popularized via "The Joy of Stats" (2010).

Ripple brickDirectly reuses the part-to-whole family's area-true math (r = k·√(value/reference)), just plotted at an (x,y) position instead of stacked. Cap the palette at two categories max; exactly one flagged bubble filled solid rust as the scene's sun. Keeps the size mismatch honest — a 3-facility operator next to a 300-facility chain shouldn't be drawn as equal dots.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS, comfortable at dozens to low hundreds of bubbles. Overlapping bubbles past that is a design problem no library fixes either — the fix is fewer, better-chosen entities.

Connected scatterplotmedium

ShowsThe same two variables as a scatter, but for ONE entity/system tracked across time, with each time-step's point joined in sequence — the reader watches the relationship itself evolve.

ExemplarHannah Fairfield's NYT "Driving Shifts Into Reverse" (2009); the Fed's Phillips Curve charts (unemployment vs. inflation, connected by quarter).

Ripple brickA single path of small circles joined by a thin SOLID line — the one deliberate exception to the usual dashed-line convention, since a solid connecting line reads as "sequence to trace." Start point is open/dim; the end point — almost always the finding — becomes the filled rust sun-circle with a leader to a mono annotation.

BuildPure SVG/CSS, mechanically simpler than it looks — a scatter plot's points given a drawing order.

Quadrant chart (2×2 framed scatter)high

ShowsA scatter plot split by two threshold lines into four named cells — sorts every entity into a plain-language bucket like "spends a lot, still fails" rather than leaving the reader to eyeball a diffuse cloud.

ExemplarThe Boston Consulting Group's Growth-Share Matrix; The Economist and FT regularly publish accountability-journalism 2x2s (e.g. "vaccination rate vs. excess deaths").

Ripple brickIdentical to the scatter brick, plus two dashed divider lines at the meaningful threshold. Each cell gets one short plain-words mono label in the corner ("MORE MONEY, MORE HARM" — the Beer Rule applies to chart labels too). Only the quadrant holding the finding gets a faint radial rust wash behind it, echoing the hero's radial-gradient sun treatment.

BuildPure SVG/CSS — literally the scatter brick plus two lines and four text labels, essentially free.

Correlation matrix / heatmap (correlogram)medium

ShowsA grid of many variables against each other, each cell colored by how strongly that pair moves together — an overview of ALL pairwise relationships at once.

ExemplarFiveThirtyEight's "Ultimate Halloween Candy Power Ranking" (2017) published a correlation table of candy attributes; the "correlogram" is standard in the R/ggplot2 world.

Ripple brickAn HTML/CSS grid, not SVG — cells shaded from bg (no relationship) to the ONE reserved accent at full saturation (strong relationship) — deliberately breaking from a diverging red/blue scale, which would smuggle a second accent color in. More Chris's own screening tool than a reader-facing scene — a grid of twenty coefficients is the opposite of "one dominant shape the eye lands on first."

BuildPlain CSS grid, fully hand-rollable, trivial even at 20x20 variables.

Scatter plot matrix (SPLOM)low

ShowsA grid of small scatter plots, one per pair of variables, showing the raw data cloud for every pair — an honesty check against Anscombe's-Quartet-style traps a single coefficient can hide.

ExemplarTraced to Tukey, formalized by Cleveland (1985); the modern reference is Observable Plot's SPLOM example. Real newsroom-published use is rare — mostly a backroom tool.

Ripple brickAn NxN grid of thumbnail scatter bricks, stripped-down axes. Should ship undecorated — no accent, no flagged sun — because by definition it has N little scenes, which structurally cannot satisfy "one sun per scene" at the whole-grid level. Internal review tool, not a reader-facing scene.

BuildHand SVG/CSS fine at small N (4-6 variables); past ~8 it's an information-density problem no library fixes either.

Parallel coordinateslow

ShowsMany variables as parallel vertical axes, each entity drawn as one line crossing all of them — lets the reader spot entities sharing a similar multi-dimensional profile.

ExemplarAlfred Inselberg, mid-1980s; the standard reference is Kai Chang's d3.parcoords. No marquee NYT/Reuters/Economist/ICIJ story built around one could be found — that thinness of real editorial use is the honest finding, not a gap in the search.

Ripple brickN vertical axes; every ordinary entity a thin polyline at low opacity so the mass reads as neutral "weather." Exactly one flagged entity's line renders full-opacity rust — the only way this chart type can honor "one sun per scene" at all.

BuildMechanically trivial SVG/CSS — the real barrier isn't construction difficulty, it's whether this is ever the right call for a reader-facing scene at all.

Radar / spider chartlow

ShowsA small number of entities compared across several named dimensions at once, each as one polygon meant to be read at a glance. Borderline-relevant to correlation proper — really a multivariate-profile comparison, grouped here with parallel coordinates for completeness.

ExemplarCommon in sports scouting (FiveThirtyEight and The Athletic player radar charts) and corporate BI dashboards.

Ripple brickIf ever used: hexagon/pentagon of hairline spokes, one entity as a filled rust polygon, never more than two overlapping shapes or the read collapses.

BuildTrivial plain SVG — but constructibility was never the blocker; appropriateness is, and it doesn't clear that bar. Actively caution against building this one — apparent "area" is an artifact of axis ordering, a bad match for a platform whose entire discipline is an honest map.

05

Geography / Where

Does WHERE something happens carry the pattern? This family answers whether harm, access, oversight, or power is concentrated in specific places, unevenly distributed across a map, or moving between places — questions only answerable because the unit of analysis is literally a location, not a name or a date.▸
Build first

Before any single chart type, there's a one-time prerequisite: a reusable, simplified, blueprint-blue basemap outline (start with U.S. states — 51 paths is tractable). Once that exists, build choropleth, then proportional-symbol map. Choropleth because it's the single most standard "geographic inequality" shape in accountability journalism. Proportional-symbol second because it reuses the EXACT area-true circle formula already solved for part-to-whole, just re-plotted onto real coordinates. Tile-grid map is a near-free bonus third build — no geometry export needed, and it solves the real "small but important states disappearing" problem. Hold off on cartogram, flow map, hex-bin, bivariate choropleth, and isochrone until a real investigation's dataset specifically calls for them.

Construction rules & brief fit for this family

Cross-cutting construction rules

Every map should read as a technical drafting exhibit on the same dark table as the rest of the page, never a bolted-on GIS widget. Basemap = schematic, not GIS render — a thin blueprint-blue stroke, fill:none, drawn like tracing paper, never a filled multi-colored map-service render. Geometry is always pre-baked — no live projection or tile library runs at runtime, matching the platform-wide build-time-not-runtime rule; a one-time script exports static SVG paths once, aggressively simplified (these are exhibits, not navigable maps). One accent still governs the data layer even though the basemap is structural blue — sequential magnitude is always a single-hue opacity ramp of the one reserved accent, never a diverging "jet" scale. One sun per scene, geographically — exactly one dominant mark the eye lands on first. Annotation reuses the existing grammar — dashed leader lines with drafting ticks, kicked to mono margin captions, no inline map labels, no hover-only tooltips.

Fit against the music brief

Fits tightly for most chart types, because "where" data mostly reuses tools already solved (area-true circles for proportional-symbol and Dorling cartograms, single-hue opacity ramps, the existing dashed-leader-line annotation grammar) rather than inventing new visual grammar. The real friction points: a bivariate choropleth is inherently two-channel (resolved by pairing the accent with neutral structural blue rather than adding a rainbow), and a fully contiguous distorted cartogram or a live-tile-service map would both read as an imported GIS tool unless the basemap stays a thin schematic line and distortion stays in the gentler Dorling-circle register.

Locator / Pin Maphigh

ShowsExact point location(s) of a specific facility, incident, address, or entity — the "here, precisely" register, proof that a systemic finding is grounded in a real, checkable place.

ExemplarJohn Snow's 1854 Broad Street cholera map is the canonical ancestor of every incident-locator map in journalism; ICIJ's Offshore Leaks / Pandora Papers interactive maps pin shell-company addresses.

Ripple brickOne static blueprint-blue outline authored once as a reusable path that every point brick in this family reuses. Each located point is a small filled circle in the reserved accent, with the same dashed leader-line-plus-drafting-tick already used in the hero graphic, kicked out to a mono margin caption. With multiple points, most render small and muted while exactly one point — the sun — gets full accent color and a labeled leader.

BuildFully hand-rolled SVG/CSS. The only non-trivial step is producing the reusable outline once; after that, plotting points is pure static arithmetic.

Dot-Density Maphigh

ShowsRaw, unaggregated count of individual events or people scattered across geography — one dot equals one (or a fixed N) real occurrence(s), deliberately un-normalized so physical volume of harm is felt.

ExemplarThe New York Times' county-level presidential-election dot-density maps — a direct descendant of Snow's cholera map, where each dot was one death.

Ripple brickSame reusable outline. Dots are tiny circles in the reserved accent at reduced opacity so overlapping dots darken naturally where density is real — an honest, un-smoothed texture. Placement computed ONCE at build time (point-in-polygon sampling weighted by each region's real count), baked into a fixed cx/cy list.

BuildMostly plain SVG/CSS, but dot-placement math is a genuine one-time build-time script — the output is static markup.

Proportional-Symbol Map (graduated symbol map)high

ShowsMagnitude of a value AT specific point locations, size-encoded — answers "which of these named places has the most/least X," fusing "where" with the already-solved area-true circle math.

ExemplarReuters Graphics' proportional-symbol maps of COVID case counts by country/state; The Economist's graduated-symbol maps of military spending or refugee populations.

Ripple brickReuses the reusable outline plus the exact area-true formula already established, just positioned at real coordinates. Fill in the reserved accent for values that matter, stroke-only ring for smaller/context values — mirroring the existing filled-circle-vs-outline-ring distinction. The largest circle is the sun.

BuildFully hand-rolled SVG/CSS, reusing existing math and outline — the cheapest genuinely new map brick.

Choropleth Maphigh

ShowsA rate or intensity value normalized per region, encoded as fill shade — "which places are worst, adjusted for size/population," the standard geographic-inequality chart.

ExemplarThe Economist's excess-mortality choropleth maps; the standard-bearer across nearly every outlet's election, health, and policy coverage.

Ripple brickThe reusable outline, every region filled using a SINGLE-HUE opacity ramp of the ONE reserved accent instead of a diverging or multi-hue scale — a real departure from typical practice, but required by the "everything neutral except the one thing that matters" rule. The single worst region gets full-opacity fill and a leader-line callout; every other region is pure shade, unlabeled.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS at runtime; the only real cost is the ONE-TIME geometry export.

Bivariate Choropleth Mapmedium

ShowsTwo variables at once, per region, via a combined color grid — "where is the danger BOTH high and unpoliced," a genuinely two-dimensional accountability question.

ExemplarJoshua Stevens' widely cited "How to Make a Bivariate Choropleth Map"; operationalized by the Urban Institute's bivariate-mapping guidance.

Ripple brickThe one map type that genuinely strains the one-accent rule, handled honestly: a 3×3 grid from the investigation's ONE reserved accent crossed with the neutral structural blue as the SECOND axis, rather than an unrelated second hue. The legend is a literal small 3×3 grid of tiny squares near the map, styled as another mono annotation block.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS — no new rendering technology, just a build-time step bucketing each region into one of 9 classes.

Cartogram (Dorling circle variant preferred)medium

ShowsDistorts region size to reflect a data value rather than true land area — "does this look different once the map stops lying about which places matter," correcting the "big empty state visually dominates" problem.

ExemplarFiveThirtyEight's cartogram-style election reweighting; the Washington Post's Dorling-style circle cartograms for county-level COVID case-count maps.

Ripple brickFavor the Dorling (circle) variant over a fully contiguous distorted shape — literally the same area-true circle math from part-to-whole, one circle per region near its true centroid. A fully contiguous cartogram reads as unfamiliar or gimmicky to a general reader; Dorling is the pragmatic middle ground for the drafting-table aesthetic.

BuildDorling: plain SVG/CSS plus a small one-time decluttering pass. A TRUE contiguous cartogram needs a real algorithm (e.g. Gastner-Newman diffusion) run once at build time; only bake the result into static SVG afterward.

Flow Mapmedium

ShowsMovement or transfer BETWEEN places — money, people, goods, permits — "where did it come from and where did it go," turning geography into a directed network.

ExemplarCharles Minard's 1869 map of Napoleon's Russian campaign is the canonical historical flow map; Reuters Graphics' 2022 flow maps of Ukrainian refugee movements.

Ripple brickThe reusable outline as background. Each flow is a hand-authored quadratic bezier arcing between origin and destination in the reserved accent, stroke-width scaled to volume, tapered toward the destination to imply direction. Only the top few flows that matter render in full color; the single biggest flow is the sun.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS for a handful to a few dozen highlighted flows — hand-authored curves are already in the vocabulary. Only breaks down at true network scale, where D3 edge-bundling becomes necessary.

Hex-Bin Maplow

ShowsSmooths a dense, noisy cloud of individual point locations into a grid of equal-area hexagonal cells shaded by count/density — for when there are too many raw points to show individually without blurring into a smear.

ExemplarPopularized broadly through Uber's open-source H3 grid system; standard across ride-share pickup-density showcases and dense GPS-ping reporting.

Ripple brickThe reusable outline as a faint reference frame. Hexagon cells tiled in a regular grid, filled at an opacity mapped from bin count — the same single-hue-ramp rule as the choropleth. Most of Ripple's source material is discrete government/corporate record data at moderate row counts, not raw continuous pings, so the overplotting problem hex-bin solves will come up less often.

BuildThe binning itself is a real computation, best done in SQL/Python at build time; once binned, rendering is plain, cheap SVG/CSS.

Tile-Grid Map (equal-unit grid map)high

ShowsReplaces true geography with one equal-size cell per region laid out in a schematic grid approximating real relative position — answers the choropleth's biggest limitation, a small state with a real problem visually vanishing next to an empty giant one.

ExemplarNPR's well-known 2016 election-results grid map replaced a standard shaded state choropleth with equal-size hexagons/squares, one per state.

Ripple brickA grid of rects/hexagons, one cell per state, positioned from a small fixed lookup table of grid coordinates. Filled with the same accent opacity ramp as the choropleth, with a two-letter mono code centered in each cell — the one map type where an in-cell label is appropriate, since there's no real geography left to annotate against.

BuildFully hand-rolled SVG/CSS, genuinely the easiest chart to build in this family — a fixed ~50-cell lookup table, not a geometry export project.

Isochrone / Travel-Time (Access) Maphigh

ShowsDraws a boundary around everywhere reachable from a point within a fixed travel time — "who can actually reach this," converting a straight-line-distance claim into a real access claim.

ExemplarThe Trust for Public Land's ParkScore mapping of 10-minute walk access to a park; CityLab/Bloomberg "care desert" mapping projects.

Ripple brickThe reusable outline as background, with soft-edged filled polygons in the reserved accent at low opacity for the reachable area, layered largest/palest to smallest/densest. The facility/service point is the sun; population OUTSIDE every ring gets a distinct treatment, since the whole point is what's left outside the shape. Echoes language Ripple already uses ("cut off from the people they happened to").

BuildNOT just SVG/CSS — the isochrone polygon is a real routing computation Ripple's warehouse doesn't natively hold, needing a one-time build-time call to a routing service. The hard part is data acquisition, not chart construction.

Small-Multiples Map Serieshigh

ShowsThe SAME map repeated many times, one per category or time slice, so change over time becomes a scanning pattern across a grid of tiny identical maps rather than a single animated or overlaid mess.

ExemplarNYT's and The Pudding's recurring use of small-multiple map grids for change over time; the cartographic application of Tufte's "small multiples."

Ripple brickReuse ONE lightweight basemap outline as an SVG symbol, instantiated repeatedly via use — avoids repeating path geometry N times. Each small map gets its own fill layer; all panels share one fixed color scale so they're honestly comparable. Exactly ONE panel (the worst year) breaks the uniform-size rule as the whole scene's sun.

BuildFully hand-rolled SVG/CSS. The symbol/use reuse trick keeps file size sane regardless of panel count.

Network / Relationships Between Entities

How are these entities tied to each other, and through what structure — direct ties, shared affiliations, or ownership/control chains — such that a reader can see who sits at the center of a web, who controls whom, and which "separate" entities turn out to be the same hand. Close to the literal center of the recon-on-power mission.▸
Build first

The org chart/hierarchy tree and the ego network. Both are fully hand-rollable plain SVG/CSS with no build-time library dependency, both slot directly into Ripple's most common story shape — one flagged subject with either a chain of control below them or a web of direct ties around them — and both consume the existing brick vocabulary (sqrt-area sizing, dashed hairlines, drafting-tick leaders, tilted captions) almost entirely off the shelf. The general force-directed node-link graph is equally high-relevance but costs more (needs a real layout algorithm at build time) — a strong second-wave build once the two priority bricks prove the family's visual language works.

Construction rules & brief fit for this family

Cross-cutting construction rules

Reuse, don't reinvent, across all nine bricks: the reserved accent fills or strokes ONLY flagged entities/ties — the neutral register carries everything ordinary. A family-wide shape grammar: circles = people/general entities, rectangles = corporate/legal entities — set once and held constant everywhere so a reader trained on one page reads the next one for free. Node/segment size always encodes a real countable quantity via the SAME sqrt-area rule already established for part-to-whole circles. One sun per scene and per page — in ego-network and org-chart bricks the sun is structurally guaranteed (the center/root IS the subject); in general node-link, chord, matrix, and dendrogram bricks the sun has to be manufactured through size + accent + label. All position-determining math happens at BUILD TIME in an offline prep script, emitting plain coordinates/paths into static SVG — no runtime physics simulation ships to the browser.

Fit against the music brief

Sits closer to the center of the creative brief than any other family — the brief's whole premise is "recon on power," and a network chart is the literal visual grammar of a power structure. The existing token/discipline system transfers onto network bricks almost without modification, which says the original three families were built on primitives general enough to carry a fourth. The one real gap the brief doesn't yet cover: a shape grammar (circle vs. rectangle) to distinguish person-entities from corporate-entities, which this family needs and which the three solved families never had to invent since they weren't drawing multiple entities at once.

Node-link diagram / force-directed graph (sociogram)high

ShowsWho is tied to whom across a whole set of entities, and how the set clusters — reveals hubs and communities. The general-purpose network chart.

ExemplarICIJ's "Power Players" tool from the Panama Papers; Bloomberg's "Connected China" (2012), a force-directed graph of CCP princelings' ties; LittleSis.org's power-mapping diagrams. The academic root term is "sociogram," coined by Jacob Moreno in the 1930s.

Ripple brickPositions computed once at BUILD TIME (spring_layout or d3-force run offline) and baked into static SVG — no live physics in the browser. Nodes sized by degree using the same area-true sqrt scale already established. Ordinary nodes fill ink-soft; the reserved accent fills ONLY flagged/implicated entities. The highest-degree node is forced to be the scene's sun — largest, near optical center, one of only a few labeled nodes; never label all nodes, that's how these become spaghetti.

BuildNeeds a real layout algorithm at build time — hand-rolling good node positions isn't practical past ~15 nodes. Once XY coordinates exist, rendering is plain SVG, no runtime library.

Ego network (radial hub-and-spoke, single-entity-centered)high

ShowsAll the direct ties of ONE named entity, radiating outward — "who does X know / who does X control," guaranteeing exactly one center.

ExemplarLittleSis.org's per-entity profile pages render exactly this. The "Kochtopus" diagram — a radial network of Koch-funded organizations, popularized in Greenpeace/DeSmogBlog reporting — is a classic accountability-journalism use.

Ripple brickLiteral hub-and-spoke SVG, pure trig, no force simulation. Center circle = the investigated entity, the largest shape on the WHOLE PAGE, filled solid in the reserved accent — the one brick where the accent gets to be a fill rather than a sparing highlight, because the whole scene's point is "this is the subject." Spokes are dashed hairline rules radiating to small ink-soft circles sized via the sqrt-area rule. A good scroll-reveal candidate: spokes draw in sequentially — "the web appears."

BuildFully hand-rollable plain SVG/CSS — spoke positions are even-angle trigonometry. A same-day build.

Arc diagrammedium

ShowsConnections between entities arranged along a single ordered line, drawn as arcs above the line — good when entities have a natural linear order and one or two ties should stand out against it.

ExemplarMike Bostock's canonical D3 arc diagram of character co-occurrence in Les Misérables; applied to legislative co-sponsorship networks ordered by ideology.

Ripple brickEntities on a single horizontal hairline baseline, ordered by a meaningful sort (dollar amount, date) always stated in a mono caption. Arcs drawn ABOVE the baseline only — never mirrored, keeping all visual weight in one register. Ordinary arcs stay hair/dim; the one or two flagged ties get the reserved accent — a single rust arc alone in a field of thin gray arcs is a strong, cheap sun moment.

BuildFully hand-rollable — arcs are simple semicircle-formula paths. No library needed if order and ties are precomputed with a small sort script.

Chord diagrammedium

ShowsTotal connection/flow volume between entities arranged around a circle — arcs sized by total volume, ribbons connecting them. Good for a SMALL set of entities.

ExemplarThe widely-republished global migration flows chord diagram (Abel & Sander, Science 2014); The Economist and Bloomberg have used chord diagrams for trade/capital-flow stories.

Ripple brickOuter ring of arc segments sized by tie volume; ribbons at low opacity, tinted toward the sending entity if directional — capped low so overlapping ribbons stay legible against the dark background. The single largest ribbon is the manufactured sun. Cap entity count hard at roughly 8 arcs, or it becomes a hairball.

BuildNeeds real build-time geometry — reaching for d3-chord's layout module at build time (to emit static numbers) is more practical than reimplementing chord geometry by hand.

Adjacency matrixhigh

ShowsA grid where rows and columns are the same entity list and a filled cell means a tie exists — the network-science alternative to node-link, specifically because it scales to dense networks where node-link collapses into a hairball.

ExemplarStandard since Jacques Bertin's "Semiology of Graphics" matrix-reordering technique; corporate-governance researchers studying interlocking directorates (the Mark Mizruchi tradition of board-interlock studies) have long used director-by-company matrices.

Ripple brickPlain CSS grid, not SVG — cheap and native. Empty cell nearly invisible against bg; filled cell ink-soft at low-medium opacity. Any row/column belonging to a flagged entity gets a full-row background wash in the reserved accent — the eye catches "this row is lit up" before counting individual cells. The densest row/column is reordered to position 1 via a simple clustering pass so related entities visually block together on the diagonal.

BuildFully hand-rollable — an HTML/CSS grid, no SVG, no library beyond a simple sort/reorder script. One of the easiest bricks in the whole family.

Dendrogram (hierarchical cluster tree)medium

ShowsA branching tree where join height encodes how similar/how far removed two entities are — "how does this set cluster" when clustering is based on measured similarity, not known category membership.

ExemplarCanonical in bioinformatics as phylogenetic trees, not honestly a journalism example on its own. The applicable investigative use: ICIJ's Offshore Leaks explorer rendered ownership chains as branching trees.

Ripple brickImportant honesty note: most Ripple "ownership chain" needs are CATEGORICAL (A owns B owns C) — that's really the org-chart brick, not a statistical dendrogram. A true dendrogram belongs where entities cluster by measured similarity (e.g. 40 nursing homes clustering by shared address/ownership pattern to expose a hidden corporate family). The join height where a flagged cluster forms gets a single accent-colored cut-line — that cut line is the scene's sun, not the tree shape around it.

BuildNeeds a real clustering algorithm at build time if used for genuine similarity dendrograms — not hand-rollable math. Rendering the resulting tree is plain SVG.

Org chart / hierarchy tree (top-down ownership or control)high

ShowsStrict top-down structure — parent owns/controls child, agency oversees sub-agency. The most literal "who controls whom" brick in the family.

ExemplarProPublica's nursing-home ownership-chain graphics; Reuters/Bloomberg's 2017 charts mapping the Trump Organization's 500+ LLCs into a top-down tree; OCCRP's Kumu.io-based ownership-chain diagrams.

Ripple brickRight-angle elbow connectors (not curved — right angles read as structural/official) in blueprint blue. Each entity a RECTANGLE — establishing a family-wide shape grammar: circles = people, rectangles = corporate/legal entities. The ultimate parent/controller at top is the sun. Ownership percentage renders as a mono tag at a perpendicular tick mid-line. A long shell-company chain should be allowed to grow visually TALL, so the reader feels "that's a lot of layers" from sheer height before reading a layer count.

BuildFully hand-rollable plain SVG/CSS — simple recursive layout math, no library needed. One of the easiest and highest-value bricks in the family.

Bipartite / two-mode networkhigh

ShowsTwo distinct entity types where ties only ever run BETWEEN the two types, never within — rendered as two parallel columns, revealing shared affiliations.

ExemplarTheyRule.net (Josh On, 2001/2011) — purpose-built to map corporate board interlocks as a draggable two-mode network of directors and companies; OpenSecrets/FollowTheMoney's donor-to-candidate networks.

Ripple brickTwo parallel columns — rectangles for organizations, circles for people, reusing the shape grammar from the org-chart brick. An entity appearing in more than one tie gets a larger node via the same sqrt-degree scaling, and if flagged, the reserved accent — sitting in the middle of a fan of lines, it becomes the obvious sun. Functionally a typed, two-column layout variant of the node-link brick.

BuildFully hand-rollable SVG/CSS — an even vertical distribution per side, no force simulation needed. Simpler to build than the general force-directed graph.

Hierarchical edge bundlinglow

ShowsNon-hierarchical relationship edges routed THROUGH an underlying tree so ties bundle along shared paths instead of crossing the canvas as independent straight lines.

ExemplarDanny Holten's 2006 IEEE InfoVis paper on software class hierarchies is the technique's namesake; popularized via D3's "flare" package-dependency example.

Ripple brickThe lowest-priority, most technically demanding brick in the family — needs BOTH a meaningful underlying hierarchy AND a separate set of non-hierarchical edges to bundle, a fairly specific data shape Ripple may rarely produce. Included for completeness, not because it's likely to ship soon.

BuildNeeds real build-time algorithmic work — would lean on d3-hierarchy's bundle() at build time. Not a quick hand-roll.

Flow / Process

As money, people, or cases move through an ordered sequence of stages, gates, or actors, where does it actually go, how much survives each gate, and where does the system quietly lose, redirect, or loop it back on itself? The mechanism of a process, not just its before/after headline.▸
Build first

Funnel chart first, Sankey diagram second. Funnel is a near-zero-risk build: a direct width-scaled extension of the "OF EVERY 100" band pattern that already exists on the live page, and attrition-through-a-system is the single most common story shape Ripple's subject matter produces. Sankey is second for build-leverage reasons — the most recognizable "flow of money/people" visual to any reader on sight, and its ribbon-drawing engine is reusable infrastructure that makes alluvial, parallel sets, and chord all cheap follow-on builds. Swimlane is the cheapest build by far (reuses ~80% existing code) and deserves an early slot too, but ranks third on mission grounds — it's a single-case receipt-carrier, not itself a systemic-pattern chart.

Construction rules & brief fit for this family

Cross-cutting construction rules

Two structural engines cover almost the whole family: a shared bezier-ribbon routine (stack nodes by cumulative value, draw tapered links) powers Sankey, alluvial, parallel sets, and chord — build it once for Sankey and the other three get cheap. The page's already-built band + dashed-hairline-divider + spine-route components power funnel and swimlane almost directly, at near-zero incremental cost. Area/length/width is always the literal value encoding so scale is felt before any percent is read. Every chart singles out exactly ONE path, bar, loop, or ribbon as the finding in the reserved accent, while everything else recedes to neutral. Because Sankey, alluvial, and chord are the densest marks anywhere in the vocabulary, density/rest needs enforcing MORE aggressively here — a quiet single-stat band should immediately follow any of the three. Two named exceptions to the one-accent rule are documented rather than hidden: alluvial's multi-category ribbons and waterfall's binary up/down bars.

Fit against the music brief

Fits cleanly for seven of the nine types using the neutral-plus-one-accent register exactly as built. Two types (alluvial, waterfall) require an honest, documented bend of the one-accent rule to a fixed small categorical or binary set — flagged rather than smoothed over, per the brief's own demand for full truth over easy polish. The family's real risk against the brief is density: Sankey and chord diagrams are structurally the busiest marks Ripple would ever build, so density-needs-rest has to be applied harder here than anywhere else, and chord diagrams specifically should be capped in entity count rather than allowed to become a hairball.

Sankey diagramhigh

ShowsHow a total quantity splits and re-splits across sequential stages, with the WIDTH of each path proportional to how much actually flowed down it — reveals both the dominant path and the leaks.

ExemplarMatthew Sankey's 1898 steam-engine energy diagram; Lawrence Livermore's annual U.S. energy flow chart; the IPCC AR6 (2022) global greenhouse-gas-flow Sankey. Directly on-mission: the opioid-settlement-money tracking shape (OpioidSettlementTracker.com / KFF Health News).

Ripple brickNodes as thin vertical bars, height = value, area-true, same discipline as the existing circles. Links: cubic-bezier ribbons from cumulative-sum stacking. ALL ribbons render dim/low-opacity except the one path that is the actual finding, which gets the reserved accent at full opacity with a drafting-tick leader. The page's existing big-numeral brick sits beside it carrying the headline total, so the Sankey itself only carries proportion, not scale.

BuildGenuinely hand-rollable for Ripple's realistic scale (2-4 stages, 3-8 nodes per stage) using cumulative-stacking math — no d3-sankey needed unless auto-layout for dozens of crossing-minimized nodes is ever required.

Alluvial diagrammedium

ShowsHow the SAME tracked population's category membership shifts across fixed checkpoints over time — a constant-size cohort being re-sorted repeatedly, not a shrinking total splitting once.

ExemplarRawGraphs/DensityDesign's canonical Titanic survival-by-class/sex/age demo; NYT has run alluvial-style charts tracking how House/Senate seat composition shifted party control.

Ripple brickSame ribbon engine as Sankey, but every column has the SAME total height (population conserved), and ribbons are colored by CATEGORY — one of two honest, documented exceptions to the one-accent rule, using a fixed categorical set from the existing token palette. One ribbon-thread still gets a halo/leader-line callout as the investigation's actual finding, so there's still one sun inside a multi-color scene.

BuildHand-rollable for a small number of columns/categories reusing the Sankey ribbon code; gets harder as category count grows, where a library's algorithmic untangling earns its keep.

Parallel setslow

ShowsHow a population splits across several DIFFERENT categorical dimensions in a chosen sequence — not a time order, a variable order.

ExemplarKosara, Bendix & Hauser's 2006 IEEE InfoVis paper "Parallel Sets"; most concretely a built-in Tableau chart type. No famous mainstream journalism piece built around it by name could be found.

Ripple brickVisually near-identical to the alluvial brick — same ribbon engine, same categorical colors — but each column represents a different VARIABLE, and column order becomes an explicit editorial choice. Nearly free once Sankey/alluvial exist, but reads more like an internal exploration tool than a publish-ready story graphic.

BuildSame as alluvial — effectively free once that exists, not worth building standalone first.

Funnel charthigh

ShowsHow many entered a process versus how many survived each successive, strictly narrowing stage — and which single stage produced the biggest drop.

ExemplarRAINN's widely reproduced "out of every 1,000 sexual assaults" criminal-justice attrition funnel; Vera Institute of Justice and Prison Policy Initiative incarceration/parole attrition funnels; the Marshall Project's "arrest to conviction" attrition graphics.

Ripple brickA stack of horizontal bars, NOT a triangular/trapezoid silhouette (wrong register for the blueprint aesthetic) — width literally proportional to count, stacked top to bottom. A direct extension of the page's already-built "OF EVERY 100 / 22 / 100" band pattern. Only the FINAL, smallest, most damning bar gets the full reserved accent.

BuildPlain CSS/SVG, no library, trivially buildable — nearly identical in difficulty to a pattern that already exists on the live page.

Flowchart / process diagramhigh

ShowsThe actual sequence of steps and decision branches a case or dollar moves through, including where it forks — an explainer of mechanism, optionally weighted by how many cases took each branch.

ExemplarThe universally recognized "How a Bill Becomes a Law" civics diagram. ICIJ's FinCEN Files (2020) published a process schematic of how a Suspicious Activity Report is filed and then mostly ignored by regulators — close to identical in intent to what this brick is for.

Ripple brickSharp/minimal-radius rects (not rounded app-UI boxes), dashed leader-line connectors with a solid arrowhead swapped in for the perpendicular tick. Attach a case count or dollar amount to every branch wherever the data supports it, letting line thickness encode volume — a flowchart with zero attached quantities risks failing the mission's own "no human on the other end of the number" test.

BuildPure hand-rolled SVG — boxes, elbow connectors, text at explicit coordinates, the exact register as the existing hero SVG. No library needed at Ripple's realistic step-count.

State transition diagrammedium

ShowsThe possible states an entity can be in, and how often it moves between them — including loops, an entity cycling back to a state it was already in, often the actual finding.

ExemplarThe classic finite-state-automaton diagram from computer science/operations research. Closest real-world use: "revolving door"/repeat-violator reporting; more common as an analyst's working diagram than a polished news graphic.

Ripple brickSmall circles/rects with uppercase mono state names, thin dashed edges labeled with transition counts. The ONE loop that is the finding gets the reserved accent, drawn as a real curved path returning to its own node, positioned slightly outside the main cluster — "this is the one that doesn't end." A strong candidate for "one sun at the whole-page scale."

BuildHand-rolled SVG, straightforward for Ripple's realistic scale of 4-8 states. No library needed.

Waterfall / bridge chartmedium

ShowsStarting from a known total, the sequence of additions and subtractions that arrive at a final total — and which single step drove most of the change.

ExemplarHome turf is corporate-earnings "EBITDA bridge" decks. The legitimate public-accountability version: federal-budget waterfall graphics (USAFacts, the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget).

Ripple brickFloating bars along a shared baseline, each starting where the previous ended. One of two places the one-accent-color rule structurally bends: up-bars (additions) sage, down-bars (subtractions) rust — up-vs-down is inherently binary. Everything else stays neutral so the sage/rust pair reads as genuinely semantic, not decorative drift.

BuildPlain SVG rects with y from a running cumulative sum — essentially arithmetic plus rect tags, no library needed.

Chord diagramlow

ShowsBetween a set of entities, how much flows each-to-each in a many-to-many web, not a one-directional funnel-down.

ExemplarCircos (circos.ca), originally a genomics tool, is the technique's namesake; the Guardian Data Blog's global migration chord diagram (~2014) is the best-known journalism use.

Ripple brickEntities as arc segments sized by total flow, chords reusing the exact bezier-ribbon technique as Sankey. Cap entity count hard at roughly 8 arcs — most of Ripple's real questions are asymmetric (money from a few sources to many recipients), not genuinely symmetric webs, so this is a narrower slice than Sankey or funnel.

BuildHand-rollable for a small, capped entity count, but genuinely the most fiddly-by-hand brick in the family — past the cap, d3-chord is the honest choice.

Swimlane / process-timeline diagramhigh

ShowsAs a single case moves through TIME, which actor or desk it's on at each point, showing both sequence and duration/stall — whose desk it sat on, and for how long.

ExemplarThe formal technique is "swimlane diagram" (Lean/Six Sigma origin). The WSJ's 2019 Boeing 737 MAX coverage ran a step-by-step regulatory process/timeline graphic showing exactly how long the FAA's approval review took at each stage.

Ripple brickHorizontal lanes reusing the page's EXISTING band component and its dashed-hairline divider verbatim — literally the same structure already used for the three coda bands on the live page. A single dotted path travels through the lanes in time order, reusing the already-coded spine-drawing function almost unchanged. Stage duration is literal horizontal distance — a long stall is a long visual gap, felt physically with no legend required.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS/JS, no library — the cheapest brick to build in this family, since ~80% of its parts already exist as working code on the live page today.

Uncertainty / Range

How sure are we? Given a number pulled from public records — an estimate, a modeled total, a rate, an extrapolated undercount, a forecast — how wide is the range of plausible truth around it, and does that plausible range still support the claim being made: does the low end still cross the line that matters, or could the real number just be noise?▸
Build first

The error bar (point-and-whisker), and the forest plot second — treat them as one build, not two. The error bar is the cheapest primitive in the entire family and is needed almost immediately the first time Ripple publishes any modeled or survey-derived number rather than a raw administrative count — skipping it risks implying false precision on the very first estimate-based finding. The forest plot is just that primitive stacked down a column against one reference line, so it costs almost nothing extra, and it's the single highest-structural-fit chart in this whole family to Ripple's actual method. Near-zero-cost bonus: the quantile dot plot is a two-color re-theme of the icon-array/tickwall already built, with no good reason to leave it unbuilt.

Construction rules & brief fit for this family

Cross-cutting construction rules

The range/band/whisker/track itself is infrastructure, not the finding — render it neutral unless the whole point of the scene is the width of doubt itself, in which case the band carries the ONE reserved accent, but only ever as a low-opacity fill, never a hard color. The moment worth the reader's attention — one point estimate crossing a threshold, one entity's interval sitting clear of the reference line — gets the single full-saturation accent hit, and only one of rust/sage-hi ever appears in a given scene. A forest plot with twelve equally-loud rows has no sun and has failed the rule. Reuse compounds more here than in any other family: the error bar IS the forest-plot row; the icon-array/tickwall IS the quantile dot plot with a two-color re-theme; the range-bar track IS the candlestick wick. The mono caption is doing honesty-work, not decoration — every band, whisker, and interval needs its confidence level, n, or margin of error spelled out nearby.

Fit against the music brief

Fits more deeply than its novelty suggests. Ripple's whole method is "does the data show it" — and honest data almost never shows a clean single number. A platform that already treats a bare null check as a trap until sampled has the exact instinct this chart family exists to serve visually: show the reader the doubt instead of hiding it behind false precision. The strongest matches (forest plot, floating range bar, bullet graph, quantile dot plot) map directly onto "look at all of them the same way" across many entities on one harm metric, with the added honesty of showing which differences are real and which are still statistical noise. The weaker matches (fan chart, spaghetti plot, HOPs, candlestick) exist to serve forward-looking model forecasts or trading-desk mechanics Ripple, as a platform auditing documented past harm, will only rarely need.

Error bar (point-and-whisker)high

ShowsA single estimate plus the range of plausible true values around it — a dot at the best-guess value with a line to the low/high bound and short perpendicular caps. The atomic unit the whole family builds from.

ExemplarFiveThirtyEight's polling-average charts; The Economist's "Graphic detail" vaccine-efficacy chart with 95% CI whiskers.

Ripple brickWhisker as a dashed leader-line, capped by two short solid drafting ticks. The point estimate is the ONE sun: a filled circle, rust if it crosses a harm/legal threshold, sage-hi if it reads healthy/resolved — never both accents in one scene. Value in Space Grotesk bold above the dot; interval in mono below ("31-46%, 95% CI"), mirroring the existing bigstat+caption pairing.

BuildFully hand-rollable at the same tier as the existing area-true circles — a line, two ticks, a circle, two text nodes.

Confidence band / ribbonmedium

ShowsA trend line over time with a shaded region around it showing the plausible range at each point — constant width for a smoothed historical series, or widening for a forecast.

ExemplarNYT's COVID-19 trend charts shading a reporting-lag/confidence band; the Atlanta Fed's GDPNow-style charts.

Ripple brickOne center-line path in ink at 2px; a second filled polygon from upper- and lower-bound points at low opacity in the ONE reserved accent — the band is the "how much doubt" fact worth the color, so the line stays neutral ink. A dashed vertical marks the observed-to-forecast handoff with a mono caption.

BuildFully hand-rollable — two paths, one filled polygon, no library at Ripple's scale.

Fan chartlow

ShowsA confidence band made explicit and nested — several bands of increasing width and decreasing color intensity fanning outward from a known past into an uncertain future.

ExemplarThe Bank of England's Monetary Policy Report "fan chart," in continuous use since 1996 — the single most famous named instance; also the U.S. CBO's deficit projections.

Ripple brickSame construction as the confidence band, 3-4 stacked polygons at descending opacities of the one reserved accent. Ripple audits what already happened rather than building forward-looking forecasts, so there is rarely a "future" for this chart to fan into — honest fit is near zero today.

BuildHand-rollable, identical cost to the confidence band, just repeated.

Floating range bar (high-low bar)high

ShowsFor each entity, a bar that floats between a low and a high value rather than starting at zero — the plausible span side by side across many entities.

ExemplarDaily weather high/low temperature bars; salary-range charts in job-market journalism, floating from a posting's stated min to max.

Ripple brickA rounded-rect spanning low-x to high-x per entity, matching the existing tick block's rounding. Filled blueprint blue for a neutral "just the facts" read, or the reserved accent if that entity's range is the finding worth flagging. Bar length must be pixel-true to the real span — same felt-scale discipline as the area-true circle.

BuildFully hand-rollable — a positioned rect per row, same tier as the existing tick elements.

Box-and-whisker plotmedium

ShowsThe five-number summary of a distribution's spread — the classic Tukey device for comparing spread across groups.

ExemplarStandard in CDC/WHO comparative reporting; The Economist has used box plots to compare income or test-score spread across countries.

Ripple brickBox as a low-opacity structural rect, median line in solid ink, whiskers reusing the error-bar's dashed-plus-drafting-tick construction. Outlier points rust ONLY if an outlier is the actual story, otherwise neutral. Overlaps heavily with the Distribution family's territory — expect it reached for less often than the range bar or forest plot.

BuildHand-rollable, no library needed — quartiles are an upstream SQL/dbt job.

Forest plothigh

ShowsMany entities' point estimates and confidence intervals stacked in one column against a vertical reference line, so the reader sees which entities' intervals cross the line (inconclusive) versus sit entirely on one side (a real, defensible difference).

ExemplarThe canonical medical meta-analysis chart from Cochrane systematic reviews; ProPublica- and Vox-style "risk of X by hospital/state" dot-and-whisker stacks.

Ripple brickThe error-bar brick repeated as rows against one solid vertical reference line. Only rows whose ENTIRE interval clears that line get colored (rust if harmful, sage-hi if safe); rows still straddling the line stay neutral dim — the brick literally renders honesty about which findings are solid versus still noise.

BuildFully hand-rollable — literally the error-bar primitive looped down a column, no library needed even at several dozen rows.

Spaghetti / ensemble plotlow

ShowsMany individual simulated or modeled trajectories overlaid as thin translucent lines, so the density of overlapping lines conveys how much independent models agree or disagree.

ExemplarThe National Hurricane Center's storm-track "spaghetti models," republished constantly during every major hurricane; the IPCC's climate-model ensemble charts.

Ripple brickA stack of thin, low-opacity lines in a NEUTRAL hue — not the reserved accent, since with dozens of overlapping lines the accumulated opacity itself becomes the color. One bold line marks the observed outcome or median run. Ripple's investigations are almost entirely about what already happened, not competing forward models — a narrow future use at best.

BuildHand-rollable for a modest line count; at real "spaghetti" scale a small generation script earns its keep, not a full library.

Quantile dot plothigh

ShowsAn uncertain quantity shown as a fixed number of individual dots, each one equally-likely outcome, colored by harm-side vs. safe-side of a threshold — turning "73% chance" into "73 out of 100 dots are rust," a countable fact instead of an abstract percentage.

ExemplarKay, Kola, Hullman & Munson, "Ten Guidelines for Better Quantile Dot Plots" (CHI 2016); dot-array "chance of rain" visualizations and FiveThirtyEight's probabilistic election explainers.

Ripple brickA direct reuse of the already-solved icon-array brick — the same tick/tick.gone grid pattern already shipped, just re-themed from solid-vs-hollow to rust-vs-sage. No new component required. Satisfies "scale felt before read" better than almost any other chart here: the reader COUNTS the harm-colored dots.

BuildTrivially hand-rollable — the existing tickwall grid with a two-color fill rule, essentially zero new build cost.

Bullet graph (threshold range indicator)high

ShowsA single measured value plotted against a background of qualitative reference ranges plus an optional target/threshold tick — "how does this one actual number sit relative to the line that matters."

ExemplarInvented by Stephen Few for business dashboards, replacing gauge/speedometer charts; echoed by EPA/CDC public dashboards plotting a pollutant or blood-lead level against action-level bands, and Flint-water-crisis-style contamination coverage.

Ripple brickA single horizontal track: 2-3 background segments in desaturated fills of increasing concern (sage to gold to rust) as context, not the finding; a thin threshold tick; and the ONE sun of the scene — a single bold marker at the entity's real measured value.

BuildFully hand-rollable — three flat rects, one tick, one marker. Simpler than the error bar.

Hypothetical Outcome Plots (HOPs)low

ShowsUncertainty shown as TIME — a short animated loop flipping through individual random draws from the underlying distribution one at a time, so the viewer experiences uncertainty the way people reason about single random events.

ExemplarJessica Hullman & Matthew Kay's academic research (CHI 2015). Adoption in mainstream daily journalism is thin, still mostly a research technique.

Ripple brickA single marker that jumps to a new position every ~600-800ms via a JS interval, with a mono counter ("DRAW 7 / 20"). Freezes on the real observed outcome at the end, with the quantile-dot-plot brick as its static/reduced-motion fallback. Reserve for a single showpiece scene, not a repeatable component.

BuildNeeds real hand-written JS beyond the static-SVG tier — meaningfully more build/QA effort than any other brick here.

Candlestick / OHLC chartlow

ShowsFor a single time period, four numbers at once — open, high, low, close — traditionally for a trading instrument's price.

ExemplarThe standard financial-markets chart on Bloomberg, Yahoo Finance, WSJ Markets.

Ripple brickIf ever needed, composes from pieces already in the family — the floating-range-bar track for the wick plus a filled body from open-to-close. Ripple should assemble it once from the range-bar primitive the rare time it's needed, not pre-build it as a bespoke brick.

BuildFully hand-rollable if genuinely needed, but no real reason to pre-build given how rarely it would get reached for.

Qualitative Evidence (Quotes, Documents, Testimony)

How does Ripple let a document or a person's own words stand as evidence — verbatim, attributed, and sourced — so the reader can judge a primary source directly, instead of taking Ripple's paraphrase of what it says?▸
Build first

The Attributed Quote Card — merge Pull-Quote and Testimony Callout into one flexible component with an optional accent-rule/attribution block, since they share almost all their CSS. It's the cheapest possible build and the one brick every future investigation needs on day one, regardless of subject matter. Second: Document Facsimile with the Redaction Overlay state built in from the start, since they share the same paper-card base. This is the brick that makes Ripple read as real accountability journalism grounded in actual paper — not just a dataviz portfolio.

Construction rules & brief fit for this family

Cross-cutting construction rules

Reserve a distinct "verbatim" type register for any directly-quoted human or document words — a serif italic, never Space Grotesk bold, which is reserved for MAGNITUDE numerals, so a reader's eye must never mistake someone's exact words for a statistic. Attribution is mandatory, not decorative, always in the established mono byline register. Redaction and highlight are opposites and must stay constructed as opposites: redaction is opaque black OVER text (suppression, never the accent color); highlight is translucent accent color BEHIND text (emphasis) — swapping these breaks the reader's ability to tell "hidden" from "important" at a glance. Documents get a literal pale "paper" surface that sits ON the dark page like a photograph — the one deliberate departure from the site's normal dark register. For a single quote or document, the normal one-sun rule holds; for a quote wall, the mass of many EQUAL, unaccented cards IS the sun at the whole-scene scale — the one brick type where sameness is the correct choice.

Fit against the music brief

Answers CLAUDE.md's one load-bearing question more directly than any other family — "who gets hurt, and does the data show it" — because it's the only family that puts an actual human, in their own words, on the page rather than an aggregate standing in for them. It's what keeps "the map is the deliverable, the case is the receipt" from being a slogan: the Quote Wall is literally the many-receipts view of a pattern, and the Testimony Callout is literally one receipt, sourced and attributed. It also matches Ripple's specific method — public records, not surveys or leaks alone — better than any other family.

Editorial Pull-Quotehigh

ShowsA single arresting sentence lifted verbatim from reporting, set large, breaking up a data-heavy passage to remind the reader there is a person or decision behind the numbers.

ExemplarProPublica's "Lost Mothers" (2017) breaks its maternal-mortality data sections with large verbatim quotes from grieving family members; NYT Magazine, The Atlantic, and The Marshall Project all use the identical convention.

Ripple brickA band-width block dropped between two data scenes, reusing the page's existing rest pause rhythm. Quote text in the verbatim serif/italic register — never Space Grotesk bold. One oversized quotation-mark glyph in low-opacity hairline color sits as pure typographic furniture, never in the accent color. Attribution runs beneath in the exact byline mono treatment. This block IS the "density needs rest" beat between two heavier scenes.

BuildPure HTML/CSS, trivial, matches exactly how the three solved families were built.

Testimony Callout (attributed quote card)high

ShowsA quote where the speaker's identity and standing IS part of the evidence — sworn testimony, an on-record interview — so attribution has to be as visually load-bearing as the words.

ExemplarCongressional-hearing and courtroom accountability coverage pulling a named witness's exact words into a standalone card with name + title + dateline — Reveal/CIR and Washington Post format, distinct from a magazine pull-quote precisely because the source's authority is the point.

Ripple brickA bordered card lifted off the page bg with a surface fill. A left rule in the ONE reserved accent color is the only accent-colored pixel in the card — it stands for "this testimony is the evidence that matters here." No headshot/avatar by default — Ripple's subjects are often people harmed by the system under investigation.

BuildPure CSS, trivial.

Document Facsimile / Exhibithigh

ShowsThe actual primary-source document reproduced so the reader sees the evidence itself, not Ripple's description of it.

ExemplarThe Intercept's "The Drone Papers" (2015) reproduces actual leaked military slide facsimiles inline; ICIJ's Panama Papers / Paradise Papers coverage embeds facsimile leaked documents directly in the narrative.

Ripple brickA "paper" card in a warm pale tone derived from ink-soft rather than the page's dark bg, reading as a physically distinct object on the dark page. Dark "ink" text inside in mono for a typed-memo look. A slight tilt-reuse rotation plus a soft drop-shadow reads as a physical object, not a UI panel. A small provenance chip carries the Bates number/FOIA ID in the neutral blueprint register.

BuildPure CSS for a styled recreation, no library. A real scanned image is just an img/data-URI — but is gated on rights, a sourcing question, not a code one.

Redaction Overlayhigh

ShowsWhat a document-holder chose to black out, used as evidence of concealment itself — the redaction is the finding, not decoration on a document.

ExemplarThe Mueller Report as published and annotated by NYT and Washington Post (2019), preserving the actual black redaction bars with marginal annotations; the FOIA-reporting beat's DocumentCloud redaction-aware annotation layer.

Ripple brickBuilt on the Document Facsimile card: spans of the "typed" text get covered with solid opaque black — the one place in the whole system near-black is used as a foreground fill, correct only sitting on the pale paper. The bars stay neutral, never the reserved accent — they are the mass, the "sun" through sheer coverage, not color. The accent is spent only on the leader line pointing at the one line that survived unredacted.

BuildPure CSS — positioned divs over text spans. A hover/click "peek" reveal is a few lines of vanilla JS.

Annotated Excerpt (line-by-line)high

ShowsA dense passage from a document, statute, or transcript broken open phrase by phrase with plain-English explanation of what each part actually means — translation, not summary.

ExemplarThe New York Times's long-running "[X], Annotated" franchise — "The State of the Union, Annotated," "The Mueller Report, Annotated."

Ripple brickThe excerpt in the verbatim serif/italic register in a wide column. From flagged phrases, a dashed leader line runs to a margin note in mono — the exact construction already coded in the hero SVG for its data annotations, walked down from chart-annotation to prose-annotation. This brick IS CLAUDE.md's own Beer Rule (plain-words translation) rendered visually.

BuildPure SVG/CSS, and already proven — reuses a construction that exists in the codebase today.

Redline / Document Diffhigh

ShowsTwo versions of the same text compared line for line — what got added, cut, or left suspiciously identical between drafts — used to prove authorship, influence, or last-minute alteration.

ExemplarProPublica's "Copy, Paste, Legislate" (2019), which used exact-text matching to show which state laws were copied nearly verbatim from lobbyist-drafted model bills.

Ripple brickBefore/After's sibling at the sentence grain, borrowing its already-solved color logic: deleted text struck-through in rust exactly like the existing struck old numeral; inserted text underlined in the reserved accent exactly like the existing new value. Two excerpts sit in a two-column band reusing the existing alternating-band component. The diff itself is computed once at build time with a plain algorithm, no runtime engine.

BuildPure CSS for rendering; the diff computation is a small build-time script, not a library or runtime dependency.

Quote Wall / Testimony Mosaichigh

ShowsMany short, individually attributed fragments from many different people displayed together so the reader feels the BREADTH of harm — a chorus, not a soloist.

ExemplarThe New York Times's front page "U.S. Deaths Near 100,000, an Incalculable Loss" (May 24, 2020), a wall of individual one-line obituary excerpts standing in for an otherwise-abstract number.

Ripple brickA CSS grid of many EQUAL-sized cards — deliberately equal, because "one sun per scene" applies at the whole-wall level: the wall as a mass is the sun, felt as density/count before any single line is read. Zero color variation across the wall except a curated handful pulled forward elsewhere. The most mission-aligned brick in the family — it's the only construction that visually proves "we counted everyone."

BuildPure CSS grid at dozens to a couple hundred quotes; true thousands-of-entries scale wants pagination, a plain engineering concern.

Statement Tracker (evolving / contradicted quote over time)medium

ShowsThe same person's or institution's own words at two or more points in time, set side by side to show reversal, walk-back, or contradiction.

ExemplarPolitiFact's "Flip-O-Meter," a named, running feature built specifically to pair an official's earlier statement against a later contradicting one.

Ripple brickReuses the existing alternating-band component exactly as built, one band per point in time, each carrying a date-mono label. The reserved accent is spent only on the specific phrase in the LATER band that flatly contradicts the earlier one — the eye is walked straight to the reversal without re-reading both quotes in full.

BuildPure CSS, trivial — literally reuses existing classes with no new construction needed.

Marginalia / Highlight Annotation (sticky-note style)medium

ShowsOne damning line inside a longer, otherwise-unremarkable document, flagged the way a reporter marks up a printout and sticks a note beside it.

ExemplarDocumentCloud's Annotations feature — the standard tool of the IRE/NICAR investigative-reporting community, built originally by NYT and ProPublica.

Ripple brickBuilt on the Document Facsimile card: the flagged span gets a translucent accent-color rectangle BEHIND the text — the deliberate visual opposite of Redaction Overlay, so "look here" and "you can't see this" never get confused. A small rotated tag reads like a handwritten flag. Overlaps enough with Annotated Excerpt that most investigations reach for one or the other, not both.

BuildPure CSS/SVG for a single flagged document image. A full scrollable multi-page interactive viewer is a materially heavier build, scoped separately if ever wanted.

Redaction-Density Scorecardlow

ShowsHow much of a released document or FOIA response got blacked out, used as a quantitative measure of an agency's opacity across many pages or requests.

ExemplarMuckRock-style FOIA-processing scorecards tracking denial/redaction rates by agency as an accountability metric in its own right.

Ripple brickHonestly, this is PART-TO-WHOLE wearing document texture, not a new construction: an icon-array using page-rectangle glyphs instead of a generic glyph — legible-cream pages vs. solid-black pages, with a cropped remainder glyph. No new brick logic is actually required.

BuildPure SVG/CSS, but it's a skin on an existing brick, not a new build.

Composition Changing Over Time

Given a whole made of several parts, how does the internal MIX — and optionally the total — move across many ordered time points, not just a single before/after split, but a multi-point trajectory: which slice grew, which shrank, and exactly when the balance tipped.▸
Build first

The 100%-stacked bar chart over time. It's the most direct answer to "the mix shifting year to year," the cheapest genuinely new geometry in the family (rows of stacked rects, no interpolation math), needs no y-axis at all so it satisfies "scale felt before read" almost automatically, and matches how Ripple's real source data actually arrives — discrete annual/cyclical public-records snapshots, not smooth continuous series. Build the absolute stacked column chart second: it reuses ~90% of the same code and adds back the "did the total also grow" dimension. Zero-cost bonus: small multiples of the already-shipped part-to-whole circle need no new build at all and can be used today.

Construction rules & brief fit for this family

Cross-cutting construction rules

Every entry reduces to one shared primitive: compute a per-segment cumulative baseline/top for each time bucket, then render that as a smooth path, a set of discrete rects, or a repeated already-solved shape. Build that stacking function once and every brick is a thin wrapper around it. Color is the biggest deliberate departure from how these charts are normally built — off-the-shelf libraries default to a fresh hue per category; Ripple's one-accent rule forbids that. Order segments by size and fill all from the neutral value ramp except exactly one reserved accent for the segment under investigation. Pick discrete columns whenever the real-world time grain actually is discrete (most of Ripple's public-records data) — never fit a smooth curve through four annual data points just because it looks nicer; that fabricates a precision the underlying records don't have, a bigger sin here than an ugly chart.

Fit against the music brief

Strong fit with one real tension to manage. The family's whole point — a mix shifting over many points — is exactly the pattern-over-time story the mission is built to surface, and the discrete, checkpoint-driven nature of most public records maps directly onto the simplest, cheapest bricks (100%-stacked bar, plain stacked column, small-multiples of the already-solved circle). The tension: this family is inherently the most visually "busy" of the ten, since a stacked chart wants many colors and labels by convention, cutting hard against the one-accent, one-sun discipline. The resolution — a neutral value-ramp for every segment except one reserved accent — is a genuine, disciplined adaptation: it forces every one of these charts to still have a single, unambiguous "sun" even when the underlying data has a dozen categories.

Stacked area chart (continuous, absolute)high

ShowsHow the absolute size of several parts of a whole moves across many closely-spaced time points, with the total height also showing the whole's total volume changing.

ExemplarOur World in Data's "Electricity Mix" stacked area chart — generation broken into coal, gas, nuclear, wind, solar, stacked continuously from the 1980s to present.

Ripple brickOne path per segment, cumulative sums per time-bucket. Segments ordered by size, filled from the neutral value ramp (ink-soft down through muted and dim) except the ONE segment under investigation, which gets rust (or sage-hi for a recovery story). No y-axis gridlines — let the rising/falling band height be felt first.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS, fully hand-rollable at real investigation scale. The only mildly nontrivial part is an optional curve-smoothing helper.

Stacked column chart over time (discrete, absolute)high

ShowsSame absolute-composition-plus-total story as the stacked area chart, but for naturally discrete time buckets where connecting them with a continuous line would fake precision the data doesn't have.

ExemplarFederal-budget/appropriations outlets (Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget, Bloomberg Government) routinely run stacked-column-by-fiscal-year spending-composition charts.

Ripple brickOne rect column per time bucket on the spacing-scale grid, each subdivided into stacked sub-rects. Same neutral-ramp-plus-one-rust-accent rule. A thin mono total-value label above each column, since column height alone can't be trusted to read precisely across a jagged multi-year total.

BuildTrivially hand-rollable — plain SVG or pure CSS flex columns, no interpolation math at all.

100%-stacked bar chart over time (normalized, discrete)high

ShowsHow the SHARE of each part changes year to year, deliberately throwing away the total-volume dimension so every column is the same height and the only thing left to read is the shifting thickness of each band.

ExemplarPew Research Center's recurring trend charts on the changing composition of the U.S. electorate and party coalitions (2024) use this exact form.

Ripple brickThe cleanest possible expression of Ripple's discipline rules. Every column always sums to 100% of panel height. No y-axis needed at all — 0% and 100% are implied, satisfying "scale felt before read" by construction. Every column neutral-ramp except the one rust band the investigation is about — because every column is the same height, that band's thickness rising or falling is the unambiguous sun with zero legend needed.

BuildFully hand-rollable, arguably the simplest new brick in the whole family — no interpolation, no curve math, just rows of stacked rects.

Streamgraph (ThemeRiver / silhouette)medium

ShowsThe same layered composition-over-time data as a stacked area chart, drawn with a centered or wiggle-minimized baseline instead of a flat one — good for conveying overall rhythm and relative volume rather than precise cumulative reading.

ExemplarThe New York Times' "The Ebb and Flow of Movies: Box Office Receipts 1986-2008" (2008), which won the Malofiej Best Graphic prize and formalized the streamgraph technique with Lee Byron and Martin Wattenberg's paper.

Ripple brickSame cumulative-sum stacking math, baseline recentered. Color discipline is the hardest fit here: the whole point is a many-layered organic flow, which fights the one-accent rule — resolved by keeping every layer neutral except a single rust current, so the eye follows one visible thread weaving through a calm, monochrome river.

BuildThe simple centered/silhouette baseline is plain-SVG hand-rollable. The true wiggle-minimizing version needs a real short algorithm most cleanly ported from d3-shape, not worth a full dependency.

Marimekko / Mekko chart (variable-width stacked column)medium

ShowsTwo dimensions of composition at once per time bucket: column WIDTH encodes a magnitude while column HEIGHT (normalized) encodes the internal mix — both "how big was the pie" and "how was it sliced" at once.

ExemplarNamed for the Finnish design house Marimekko, popularized as a strategy-consulting tool by McKinsey, BCG and Bain — home turf is the consulting deck, not a dense journalism time series.

Ripple brickExtends the 100%-stacked-bar brick by scaling each column's width LINEARLY to that year's total magnitude (not sqrt — a single 1-D axis). An explicit mono total underneath each column reinforces the felt width, since width-ratio judgment is harder for readers than height-ratio judgment.

BuildPlain SVG/CSS, hand-rollable — identical difficulty to the 100%-stacked bar plus one extra linear width calculation.

Small multiples of the existing part-to-whole circles (snapshot grid)high

ShowsComposition at a handful of distinct time checkpoints shown as a repeated row of separate small shapes rather than one continuous trend — trades continuous-trajectory legibility for easy side-by-side comparison when only a few checkpoints exist.

ExemplarPew Research Center and Reuters Graphics both run this constantly for demographic and generational composition comparisons at a handful of years/decades.

Ripple brickZero new geometry: this is the already-solved part-to-whole area-true circle brick called N times in a row along the existing full-width alternating-band layout, one per checkpoint, tilted mono year caption beneath each. Public records usually only exist AT a few checkpoints anyway, so this will likely be the default "quick composition-over-time" brick reached for before anyone builds a full stacked area.

BuildTrivially hand-rollable — the existing, already-shipped brick invoked repeatedly. No new math, no new component.

Polar area / coxcomb / Nightingale rose diagramlow

ShowsComposition changing across an ordered or cyclical set of wedges arranged radially, where each wedge is itself subdivided by category, with wedge radius sometimes also carrying a magnitude.

ExemplarFlorence Nightingale's 1858 "Diagram of the Causes of Mortality in the Army in the East" — the founding, canonical reference in all of dataviz teaching, built specifically to prove to Parliament that soldiers were dying of preventable neglect.

Ripple brickPolar-coordinate wedges arranged in a circle, each stacked radially like a bent stacked bar. Historically resonant for Ripple specifically (a whistleblower-style chart forcing an audience to see a harm pattern it had ignored) and worth having for a single feature-moment homage, but the same literature documents readers compare polar areas less accurately than plain stacked bars — an occasional special-occasion brick, not a workhorse.

BuildPlain SVG, no library required, but meaningfully fiddlier than any other entry — real angle/radius bookkeeping and arc large-flag logic.

Alluvial diagram (entity-level composition flow) — boundary casemedium

ShowsNot just each stage's aggregate composition in isolation, but where the mass actually MOVED FROM and TO between a few time snapshots — e.g. of the market share Company X lost, how much went to Company Y versus a dozen smaller competitors.

ExemplarRAWGraphs, the open-source tool that made alluvial diagrams accessible to journalists, describes them as ideal for "changes between categories on 2 moments in time"; used in financial-flow investigative work.

Ripple brickGenuinely straddles this family and the flow/network family: the stage columns are exactly the 100%-stacked-bar brick above (reuse directly), connected by curved ribbon paths sized by flow magnitude. The STAGE COLUMNS belong to this family; the RIBBON-DRAWING geometry is closer to the flow/network family's Sankey engine and should be reused from there, not re-derived.

BuildThe stage-column half is plain SVG/CSS (already covered). The ribbon half needs real hand-geometry — doable by hand (~50-80 lines) but nontrivial; reuse the flow-family's ribbon math rather than re-deriving it twice.
