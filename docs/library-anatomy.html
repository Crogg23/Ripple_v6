Three ways to draw the Library.
Pick one and I'll build it.

This is not a finished build — it's a choice. Three genuinely different ways to organise the same 368 charted tables, 2,694 verified joins and 2,454 pipeline objects. Each sketch below is live, running on the real data, laid out by the real compiler. They differ in the one thing that matters: what they use as the organising idea — sequence, identity, or place. Every number on this page was measured from files on disk this session.

First: what the Library actually is

measured, not assumed

Before choosing a metaphor, here's the shape of the thing. It is not a thousand-node hairball. It's a wide, shallow refinery: an enormous intake floor, a translation layer that is almost exactly 1:1 with it, and a small set of finished shelves. The depth is only four steps at its deepest.

That flow line is the answer to "sequential interaction" and nobody had drawn it before — it comes out of the dbt lineage graph (target/manifest.json), which no previous pass had opened. The three paradigms below each take a different position on how much of it to show.

The three words, and which graph answers each. They are three different graphs over the same tables, at right angles to each other.

STRUCTURE — what exists, how it groups. The subject rooms and row counts.
FUNCTIONAL INFRASTRUCTURE — what each part's job is. The five stages, and which ID families a table carries.
SEQUENTIAL INTERACTION — what feeds what. The 1,366-edge lineage DAG, and the handful of places many sources converge into one.

A paradigm that only draws one of the three is a third of an answer. Each below leads with one axis and carries the other two as overlays. That's the trade you're picking between.

A · The Refinery

organising idea: sequence

Material enters on the left and leaves on the right as finished shelves. Position on the x-axis is time. If you can read a factory floor diagram, you can read this — and the one question it answers instantly is "where does this number come from, and what happens to it next?"

hover a cell — its whole chain lights up
2,454 objects · 1,366 lineage edges · five stages, left to right in run order.

What it's strongest at

  • Sequence, completely. It's the only one of the three where run order is the geometry itself.
  • Provenance in one hop. Click a shelf, the chain back to raw intake lights up. That's the "show your work" claim made literal.
  • Honest about proportion. You can see at a glance that the intake floor dwarfs everything else, and that the desk at the end is three objects wide.

What it costs

  • The join web has nowhere to live. The 2,694 identity links run sideways across the columns and turn to spaghetti. They have to become an on-demand overlay, not a default.
  • Subject grouping gets weak. Health and Environment sit side by side in the same bank with nothing but a label between them.
  • It reads as machinery, not as a library. Correct, but colder than the mission's voice.

Interaction paradigm

Camera is rails, not free-flight — it slides along the x-axis and the zoom is anchored to a stage band, so you can never get lost; there's always a stage under you. Semantic zoom: bank → sub-column → cell → card. Selecting a cell dims everything not in its lineage to ~8% and animates the chain in run order, upstream first, ~40 ms per hop — so you watch the order rather than read it off a legend.

B · The Constellation

organising idea: identity

Tables fall toward the ID families they carry. A table that only has an NPI sits inside the NPI well. A table carrying both NPI and EIN is pulled into the gap between them — and those in-between tables are the whole point, because they're how a question crosses from one world into another. Position is an argument, not decoration.

ladder: turn tiers off to pull the spine out of the noise
368 charted tables · 2,694 verified joins · 14 ID wells.

What it's strongest at

  • It answers the mission's question. "Can I get from a doctor to a company to a place?" is a spatial question here — you trace it with a finger.
  • Solves the hairball honestly. Turning off the two weakest tiers drops 1,517 of 2,694 edges and the STEEL spine stands up on its own. That's the core UX problem named in the design brief, solved by the layout rather than worked around.
  • Bridges are visible by position alone. The tables in the middle are the valuable ones and you don't need a legend to find them.

What it costs

  • Sequence is gone. Nothing about run order survives; it becomes a badge on a card, not geometry.
  • Weakest at "what does this table do." Function collapses into "which key does it carry," which is only part of the job.
  • Learnable, not instant. Someone new needs one sentence of explanation before the picture means anything.

Interaction paradigm

Camera is free orbit with magnetic snap — release near a well and it settles into that well's frame. Semantic zoom: wells → well + members → table + neighbours → the join itself, and at the deepest step the edge stops being a line and becomes the actual matched-column pair with a sample of matched values. Tier visibility is the primary control and it lives in the chrome permanently, never in a menu — it's the honesty dial.

C · The Stacks

organising idea: place

It's called the Library, so draw a building. Subject rooms sized by how much is filed in them; inside each room, one shelf per table, sized by how much is on it. Nothing ever moves between builds — so after a week you know where things live the way you know a real building, and that muscle memory is the feature.

hover a shelf
368 shelves in 34 rooms · room size = tables filed · shelf size = rows (log).

What it's strongest at

  • Structure, immediately. One glance gives you the whole subject balance of the Library — Health is a wing, Housing is a closet.
  • Zero-instruction legibility. Everyone already knows how a floor plan works. No metaphor to teach.
  • Scales past 1,000 without changing. Treemaps don't degrade the way node-link layouts do; 5,000 shelves would look like the same building.
  • Stable coordinates. A table is a place you can name, remember, and link to.

What it costs

  • Both graphs become overlays. Neither the joins nor the lineage are in the geometry — they're drawn on top, and on top of a treemap that's genuinely hard to keep readable.
  • It hides topology. You can't see that a table is a hub; you have to be told.
  • Depends on the subject axis being good — which it now is (342 of 368 resolved), but it wasn't until this session fixed it.

Interaction paradigm

Camera is a proper map: pan and zoom, no rotation, always north-up — the least cinematic of the three, deliberately, because a building you can rotate is a building you get lost in. Semantic zoom is where the drama lives: floor plan → room → shelf → the spine of the book, and each step swaps in a different amount of text rather than just scaling the same marks. Join threads arc above the plan like wires over a street, so the plan stays readable underneath.

How to choose

the actual trade

Scored against the three words in the brief. Nothing here is a tie-breaker on looks — they'd all look good. The question is which axis you want to be free, because whichever one is the geometry costs nothing, and the other two cost interaction.

If you want my read — and it's yours to overrule: C as the home screen, B as the second screen, A as a drill-down. Stacks is what you open to know what you have; Constellation is what you open when you have a question that crosses subjects; Refinery is what you open when you doubt a number. They're not actually exclusive — they share one compiled payload and one renderer, so building a second one after the first is a fraction of the cost of the first.

The tech stack, for all three

Same answer regardless of which you pick, because the hard part isn't the drawing — it's that the thinking happens at build time. All layout is compiled in Python into one JSON payload and inlined; the browser only draws. That's constitution §7 (AI/compute is build-time, not a runtime dependency) applied to graphics, and it's why this page opens instantly with no server and no network.

  • Canvas 2D, not SVG, not WebGL. 2,454 marks and 2,694 edges is comfortably inside Canvas 2D's range. SVG dies at this node count (one DOM node per mark); WebGL buys headroom you don't need and costs a toolchain you'd have to maintain. If it ever passes ~50k marks, the upgrade path is WebGL instancing without changing the payload.
  • Batched paths. Edges are grouped by tier into a handful of prebuilt Path2D objects and stroked once per group — ~6 draw calls for thousands of lines instead of thousands of calls. Already proven in deepfield.html.
  • Coalesced redraws. Every input schedules at most one repaint per animation frame, so a fast mouse can't queue up work.
  • Deterministic layout. Seeded PRNG, no wall clock; same inputs give a byte-identical file. Position becomes memory.
  • One HTML file, no dependencies. Double-click to open, works offline, on a plane, behind a firewall.

Two things I changed along the way

yellow lane · receipts
  • Fixed the subject axis. The domain label on the connect graph is 62% other — useless as an organising axis, and the reason an earlier pass gave up on it. A table's real subject is the schema of the mart built from it, which you get by walking the dbt DAG downstream. That resolves 342 of 368 tables to a real subject instead of 140. Paradigm C is only possible because of this.
  • Made the confidence ladder ordinal. The inherited palette gives each tier a different hue, so a weak link reads as merely different rather than weaker. It's now one hue, brightest at STEEL, dimming down the ladder — so the honest encoding rule is enforced by the ramp itself. Meaning and ordering are untouched (design brief D2). Both ramps were run through the palette validator: monotone lightness, visible step gaps, dimmest step clears contrast on this surface.
