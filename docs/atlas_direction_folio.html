The Library Atlas
a folio of seven directions

Seven genuinely different ways to draw the same true thing. Every plate below is a concept sketch drawn from the real warehouse data — real counts, real proportions, nothing invented. None of them is built yet. Choosing is the point.

Law one — the ladder

Proof comes in six grades, from “same government ID number” down to “names look alike.” A weaker link may never be drawn louder than a stronger one. Every direction here obeys that by construction — ink, light, or thread bound to the rung, with no override.

Law two — presence first

675 datasets haven’t been through link discovery yet. They hold 2.4% of the library’s rows. Every plate keeps them quiet, honest, and labeled “queued” — background, never headline. The story is what the library has.

How this folio was made: seven designers each developed one direction from a different form family, then seven adversarial judges tried to kill each one — checking both laws, pushing the real quantities through every proposed form, and re-verifying the numbers against the warehouse. All seven survived, each with mandatory fixes, which are already reflected in the plates and noted honestly below. The scores are the judges’, out of five.

The plates run in order of temperature — from perfectly still print to a camera flying through a building. Read them in any order.

Plate IThe printed atlas · editorial stillness

The Survey Plates

The government atlas of your own library — six still, print-grade pages, every number real, nothing to click and nothing missing.

What it is

Six composed pages, the way the 1970 National Atlas did it: cream paper, engraved type, one page states one truth and just stands there. What’s on the shelves. What languages the files speak to each other. How strong the wiring is. How the machine narrows from 1,110 raw files to three desks. Where the busy junctions are. And an index of all 155 connected files you can actually pore over. You turn pages. Nothing spins.

Why it might win

In a stack of spinning dashboards, stillness is the pattern-break. The pitch is confidence itself: this database was worth printing. Every caption carries a checkable number, so five minutes of reading builds compounding trust. And the ladder page enforces law one with the oldest device in print — stronger proof is literally darker ink, welded to the rung.

The build

matplotlib composing print-dimension SVG pages, one readable Python function per plate, bound into a single scroll-snap HTML. Medium risk — the hard part is typography, not code.

What the kill-test said

Passed both laws cleanly. Demanded the ink ramp carried into the two summary pages, and an honest note on the cover page: the top 31 tables hold 90% of all rows, so the cover aggregates and says so.

Honest weakness

Nothing to click. A still page has nowhere to hide — at 90% typographic execution it reads as a handsome poster, not an atlas.

Plate IIThe grid, not the graph · matrix forms

The Concordance

Every proven connection printed on one re-sortable page — the darker the ink in a cell, the harder the proof.

What it is

The mileage chart from the back of an old road atlas, grown up. The 155 connected files run down the side and across the top; where two of them cross, an inked cell says a proven connection exists — and how hard the proof is, by size and darkness alone. Four buttons re-sort the same page into four different true stories: by neighborhood, by language, by agency, by grade. Press one and every cell slides to its new crossing.

Why it might win

This is the one plate in the folio already drawn from the real data — the health block, the environment block, and the two hub stripes are actually there, found by the ordering algorithm. Anyone can draw a network hairball; people who know the literature know the reorderable matrix is the professionally-correct cure, and almost nobody makes one beautiful. Best cold-open stat in the deck, printed honestly: 2,694 verified links across 2,371 crossings — survivors of 2.66 million tested pairings.

Pure Plotly scatter plus a seriation step (visible scipy, swappable), exporting to one standalone HTML with animated re-sorting. Medium risk — the craft is making the block structure crisp, and it already looks crisp above.

Plate IIIThe cross-section · an engineer’s cut

Section A–A′

The library cut open the way the Army Corps draws a dam — and one narrow waist where all the combining squeezes through four pipes.

What it is

An engineer’s sectional drawing of the machine. Raw files pour in at the top (1,110), get filtered (975), and everything that welds several sources together passes through exactly four named pipes before 362 finished shelves and, at the very bottom, three desks where a person signs. Links never appear as a spiderweb: they’re a rated joint schedule in the margin — strongest bolts first — and twenty labeled conduits. Hover any stone for its specimen tag; click one and only its joints draw.

Why it might win

The pipeline hourglass is the single most architectural fact in the data, and a section drawing is the form invented for exactly this: showing a structure’s narrow point. A cream engraved plate in a stack of dark dashboards reads as engineering seriousness like nothing else in the folio.

The build

A deterministic Python geometry compiler feeding Plotly — the same compile → app → export pattern the repo already uses. Medium risk; the make-or-break is batching the engraving into a few traces.

What the kill-test said

Caught two would-be lies before they shipped: the chamber counts are build-objects, not datasets (now labeled as architecture, not inventory), and the waist needed spillways — most tables flow around the four pipes; only cross-source welding goes through. Truer, and a better dam.

Honest weakness

Lives or dies on drafting polish. At 80% execution the blueprint conceit collapses into themed clip-art.

Plate IVThe schematic · transit-map discipline

The System Map

A subway map you already know how to read: every ID system a colored line, every dataset a station, a join is just a journey.

What it is

Strong government IDs are the rail lines — the NPI line, the EIN line, the CCN line, each colored and named. Datasets are stations; a file carrying two rare IDs is an interchange with the classic white ring. The 1,242 name-and-zip matches are the street grid running quietly under the rails — reach, not rail. Two downtowns emerge because the data says so, wired together by the EIN trunk. A departure board on hover, service-level buttons that add grades strongest-first, and a rail-yard inset for the pipeline.

Why it might win

Everyone on earth reads a transit map in two seconds — instant legibility plus instant charm, and “which lines serve this station” is word-for-word the real question: which IDs can this file join on? The only direction the judges scored a perfect five on the cold open. And the compression is honest: they verified the per-ID clusters really are near-cliques, so drawing them as lines is truth, not decoration.

The build

Plotly as a canvas, NumPy/shapely drawing the geometry, and a hand-editable YAML of station positions. The code is medium; the labor is high — octilinear beauty is hand-layout, and every data refresh means re-curating it.

What the kill-test said

Demoted FIPS and ZIP to street grammar — they’re place-only rungs, and law one outranks charm. Lines show verified journeys only; the street grid rides as bundled corridors, not 1,242 drawn segments.

Honest weakness

The beauty is handmade. This is the direction you marry, not date.

Plate VThe continuous field · no dots at all

The Loom

The whole library woven into one bolt of cloth — every thread a verified link, and the surer the match, the tighter the weave.

What it is

No dots anywhere. The 23 ID systems are the warp — the long threads strung on the frame. Every verified link is one pass of the shuttle. The surer the match, the tighter and darker the weave: 806 same-ID picks make a dense indigo satin; 1,246 name-and-place picks make the wide, looser plainweave; place-only matches are open gauze at the edge. One gold thread — the library’s single industry-code-grade link. The 82 searched-but-unmatched hang as bare threads past the hem; the 675 not-yet-run wait as coiled roving at the selvage: stock, not failure.

Why it might win

It’s the most artistically original thing in the deck, and the strongest pure-engineering flex: this isn’t a chart library driven hard, it’s a small renderer — NumPy compositing, supersampling, levels of detail — in plain Python someone can read. The kill-test also surfaced the deepest insight of the whole exercise here: each thread system speaks at exactly one confidence — the language a link speaks is its grade — and the cloth now wears that fact as its left-to-right gradient.

The build

A NumPy procedural renderer under a Plotly image with an invisible hover grid; Dash drives the loupe and the band-lift. The highest-risk build here: it’s all-or-nothing on texture quality, and the export needs a hard size budget.

What the kill-test said

Re-laid the cloth around the key-equals-grade discovery, made every interaction geometric instead of dimming (law one holds in every state), and lowered the bar from photoreal to stylized-tactile so an honest 90% still reads as a made thing.

Honest weakness

If the cloth illusion misses, it’s a fuzzy striped heatmap. No cheap fallback exists — the illusion is the piece.

Plate VIThe literal library · warm and tactile

The Sewn Library

It’s called the Library — so draw the library. Books on walnut shelves, and every proven connection is actual thread sewn between spines.

What it is

Every dataset is a book — fat spine, more rows. Every proven link is bookbinder’s thread: doubled and heavy when the ID numbers match, a faint dotted stitch when it’s only “same place.” A framed sampler on the wall shows the six stitch grades with their real counts — including one lonely single stitch, and a bare last row penciled “a person must check.” Twenty-three thread spools sit on the bench; NAME@ZIP is the fat one, nearly half the sewing in the room. New arrivals wait wrapped on a cart, queued for cataloguing. Pull a book and its threads fan out across the wall.

Why it might win

Warmth and wit are the rarest things in a data-viz portfolio, and this is the only piece in the genre with a light, lamplit thumbnail — unmistakable in a wall of dark-mode demos. The metaphor is a literal reading of the platform’s own name, executed with craft instead of as a joke. Art direction as an engineering skill.

The build

Art-directed Plotly — a bookshelf is honestly a bar chart wearing wood — with NumPy bezier threads. Medium risk; the real cost is illustration polish, because skeuomorphism has no middle ground.

What the kill-test said

Re-bundled the resting cords by grade so the fattest bundle can’t be the loudest weak thing; locked all spool hues to one lightness band so color never fights grade; tamed the 1,242-thread fan to aggregate-first.

Honest weakness

Shelf position carries charm, not information — a Tufte purist calls it ornament, and they wouldn’t be entirely wrong.

Plate VIIThe built object · cinematic 3D

The Narrows

A five-floor stone archive at dusk, pinched at the waist — and every wire between shelves glows exactly as bright as its proof.

What it is

The whole warehouse as one building you fly through. Crates arrive on the ground floor, get tidied a flight up, and then the tower pinches: only four small rooms do the combining. Above them, the ready shelves; on the roof, one lit lantern room with three desks — because at the top of this machine, a person signs. The wiring between shelves glows by its proof: filament-white for same-ID, down to a faint ember for same-place — and nearly half of it runs through one fat, deliberately dim name-and-zip tray. Five chapters of camera tour, then free orbit, hover, and hub flares.

Why it might win

A confident camera move is the fastest “whoa” in the business, and nobody expects one from a plain Python file. The pinched silhouette tells the whole story before a single label loads. The judges called it the strongest résumé signal in the deck: spatial graphics engineering — scene, lighting, bundling, choreography — in a stack it wasn’t obviously built for.

The judges’ scores

The build

Plotly 3D with precomputed camera frames and pre-baked shading in Python. Medium-high risk: the waist bundling is the hardest single piece of geometry in the folio, and Plotly has no real lighting — cinema by choreography, not gloss.

What the kill-test said

Stopped the hero caption claiming flow the data refutes (“only four rooms do the combining” is the true sentence). Floors moved to square-root scale so the waist physically fits its own drama. Glow switched from transparency to luminance, so a pile of dim wires can never out-shine the white ones.

Honest weakness

The most scaffolding ink of any direction, and the least graceful failure mode: badly bundled, the waist is glowing spaghetti.

The studio’s read

Which one becomes real is a taste call, and it’s yours. For what it’s worth, here’s what we’d hang on the wall — three picks that cover three different kinds of first impression.

The charmer

IV · The System Map

The only perfect cold-open score, the rarest skill signal, and the metaphor answers the platform’s actual question. Cost is honest: the beauty is hand-laid, and refreshes mean re-curating. Pick it if the Atlas should make strangers smile before it makes them think.

The sure thing

II · The Concordance

Already provably real — its plate above is the actual data. Best craft-per-hour in the folio, exports to one file, and reads as deep form literacy to anyone who knows the field. Pick it if the Atlas should quietly outclass every hairball it’s compared to.

The swing

VII · The Narrows

The strongest résumé signal and the biggest “whoa,” at the biggest risk. If the waist bundling lands, nobody forgets it. Pick it if the Atlas should be the thing no one believes came out of a Python file.

And the other four, in one honest line each: the Survey Plates pair beautifully with the existing dark app as pure range — engineer and art director; Section A–A′ is the pick if the audience is engineers specifically; the Loom is the most original object here and the biggest craft gamble; the Sewn Library is the one people would screenshot and share.

These aren’t mutually exclusive forever — several would happily live as pages of one larger piece someday. But the next step is one prototype, built properly, of whichever one of these makes you want to open it tomorrow.
